# Research brief: Why credit cards protect you from fraud better than debit cards
Explainer (Ticker), posts Sunday Dec 27, 2026, 12 PM CT. Researched Oct 28, 2026. Evergreen: rules below are federal regulations in force as of Oct 2026.
Angle: not "who pays in the end" but WHOSE money is missing while a fraud claim is investigated. (Oct 24 Hot Take already covered Reg E $0/$50/$500 tiers; keep that to one line at most.)

## Core facts
1. Credit cards: Truth in Lending Act (as amended by the Fair Credit Billing Act), Regulation Z 12 CFR 1026.12(b): cardholder liability for unauthorized use is capped at $50 (and $0 if reported before any use). FTC consumer page "Lost or Stolen Credit, ATM, and Debit Cards": if only the card NUMBER is stolen (not the card), liability is $0. FTC enforces the FCBA per Cornell LII.
2. Credit card disputes, Regulation Z 12 CFR 1026.13 (CFPB official commentary, interp 13): until the billing error (including an unauthorized charge) is resolved, the consumer need not pay the disputed amount or related charges; the creditor may not try to collect it or report it as delinquent. Creditor must acknowledge within 30 days and resolve within two complete billing cycles (Cornell LII says not to exceed 90 days).
   -> During the dispute the missing money is the issuer's credit line, not the cardholder's cash.
3. Debit cards: Electronic Fund Transfer Act / Regulation E, 12 CFR 1005.11 (error resolution):
   - Bank must investigate and decide within 10 business days of notice, OR provisionally credit the account within 10 business days and take up to 45 calendar days to finish.
   - For point-of-sale debit card transactions, foreign-initiated transfers and new accounts: up to 90 calendar days (1005.11(c)(3)(ii)); provisional credit still due within 10 business days (20 business days for new accounts). Sources: eCFR 1005.11 (current as of July 2026), Cornell LII, Fed Consumer Compliance Outlook (2026, first issue), 1998 Fed final rule.
   - Bank may withhold up to $50 of provisional credit for an unauthorized transfer (Reg E liability amount).
   - If the bank requires written confirmation of an oral notice and doesn't get it within 10 business days, it can take the full 45 days without provisional credit.
   -> Meanwhile the stolen money is the consumer's own checking cash.
4. Reg E liability tiers (one line only; covered Oct 24): $0/$50/$500/unlimited depending on lost/stolen and reporting speed (FTC, CFPB).
5. Card network zero-liability: Visa's Zero Liability policy covers unauthorized transactions on Visa-network credit AND debit cards, excluding ATM transactions, PIN transactions not processed by Visa, and certain commercial cards (issuer disclosures; Visa). Mastercard has a similar Zero Liability policy (general knowledge, not separately verified this session; script says only "card networks' policies often cover debit too").
6. Knock-on effects while debit cash is missing: payments drawn on the account (autopays, checks) can be returned or trigger overdraft fees. CFPB has flagged overdraft and returned-item fee practices (e.g. 2022 guidance on depositor fees; Dec 2023 Atlantic Union Bank $6.2M order on overdraft fees). This is a general mechanism, not a specific stat.

- 2025: consumers reported $15.9 billion lost to fraud, ~3 million fraud reports (FTC testimony to Joint Economic Committee, March 2026; FTC press releases 2026 say "about $16 billion", up ~25%).

## Worked example (hypothetical)
Checking balance $2,500. Thief makes $2,000 of unauthorized debit purchases. $2,500 - $2,000 = $500 left in checking until the bank provisionally credits (up to 10 business days) or resolves the claim. A $1,200 rent autopay (example) in that window would overdraw the account. Same fraud on a credit card: the $2,000 sits on the issuer's line, disputed; cardholder doesn't have to pay it during the dispute and checking still has $2,500.

## Surprise fact
For many debit card purchases (point-of-sale), a bank can take up to 90 days to finish investigating, not 45. And network zero-liability policies often cover debit too: the real gap is cash flow during the investigation, not the final bill.
