## The story: borrowing costs hit multi-decade highs (week of Sept 28-Oct 1, 2026)
### Treasury yields
- 10-year Treasury yield touched about 5.34% (as high as 5.342%) on Thursday, Oct 1, 2026: highest since 2002, above its 2007 peak (Reuters via Investing.com/Yahoo Finance; CNN Business Oct 1, 2026; Bloomberg). It eased to about 5.24-5.26% later Oct 1.
- Closed Sept 30, 2026 at about 5.29-5.30% (session high above 5.30%) (treasuryratewatch / helious summaries of Treasury data).
- Mid-Sept: 10-year rose as high as 5.04% on Sept 15, 2026, closing at 5.00%, highest since 2007 (CNBC Sept 15, 2026; CNN Sept 14). Sept 26: 5.23% (CNBC).
- Late February 2026, at the start of the Iran war, the 10-year was below 4% (CNBC Sept 23/26, 2026). Rise of more than 1 percentage point since.
- 30-year Treasury yield: 5.48% on Sept 24, 2026 = highest since 2004 (Bloomberg); breached 5.5% after Iran ceasefire conditions rejected; reached 5.683% on Oct 1, 2026 per Tradeweb data cited by WSJ, a 24-year high (Quartz Oct 1, 2026).
- History: 10-year first crossed 5% since 2007 in October 2023 (as high as 5.029%) (CNBC Oct 19, 2023).
- Global: Japan 10-year crossed 3.00% on Sept 2, 2026 for first time since 1996; 3.10% on Oct 1, 2026. UK 10-year gilt 5.25% (highest since 2008) and Germany 10-year 3.35% (highest since 2011) in the Sept 2026 global selloff (BNN Bloomberg Sept 1, 2026; Trading Economics).
- Why (attributed): oil-driven inflation from the US-Iran war and Strait of Hormuz closure; investors pricing more Fed hikes; heavy government bond issuance / deficits; strong growth and an AI-fueled investment boom (CNBC Sept 23 & 26, 2026; Fortune Sept 14, 2026; Reuters Oct 1, 2026).

### Worked example (hypothetical, own math; standard amortization, principal and interest only)
- $400,000 loan, 30-year fixed: at 5.98% = $2,393.06/month; at 7.28% = $2,736.85/month. Difference $343.79/month, about $4,125/year.
- Total interest over 30 years: about $461,502 at 5.98% vs about $585,266 at 7.28% (about $123,764 more).

## Who wins / loses (descriptive)
- Lose: home buyers and anyone with variable-rate debt (credit cards, HELOCs), people refinancing, holders of existing bonds (prices fall when yields rise), the federal budget (interest costs).
- Win: savers in high-yield savings, CDs and T-bills; new bond buyers lock in higher yields; homeowners with old low fixed rates are unaffected (but "locked in").

## Bond basics (for the board)
- A yield is the return a bond buyer gets. When new bonds pay more, old ones paying less must sell at lower prices. Price and yield move opposite.
- 30-year mortgage rates tend to track the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread (Oct 1: 7.28% vs about 5.3%, about 2 points).
