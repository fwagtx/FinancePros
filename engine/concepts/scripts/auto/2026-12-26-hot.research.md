# Research brief: 2026-12-26-hot (Hot Take / Money Myth)
Myth: "Paying your card in full every month doesn't build credit."
Fact: It does. Paying in full on time feeds payment history and keeps amounts owed low. The twist is WHEN issuers report:
usually the statement balance at/after the statement closing date, not the due date, so even pay-in-full users can show high utilization.
Fresh angle (NOT the Oct 6 "carrying a balance / interest" video): reporting timing and utilization.
Research date: October 27, 2026. Posts December 26, 2026. All facts below are evergreen definitions/guidelines; dated data is labeled.

## Key facts (with sources)
1. FICO score weights (general population), per myFICO "What's in my FICO Scores?": payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%,
   length of credit history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%. myFICO notes the weights are for the general population
   and may differ by profile. Amounts owed includes the proportion of revolving balances to credit limits (utilization).
   myFICO: owing money "does not necessarily mean you are a high-risk borrower"; using a lot of available credit "may indicate that you are overextended."
2. Reporting timing, myFICO support article "Why does my credit report show balances when they're paid off each month?":
   "Each month, your credit card issuer reports the outstanding balance appearing on your last billing statement to the credit bureaus.
   This means that whether you pay your balance in full each month or just partially, the balance appearing on your credit report
   for an account will usually be the balance appearing on your last statement."
   myFICO myth-busting blog ("You Don't Need to Carry Credit Card Balances to Improve Your FICO Scores"): "Even if you pay your balance in full
   by the due date, the reported balance won't change because it was reported before you made your payment."
   Experian (Ask Experian): issuers usually report balance and limit around the end of each statement period; the utilization factored
   into the score is based on the balance recorded on the statement closing date, even if paid in full by the due date.
   Caveat: practices vary by issuer (some report on a fixed day); reporting lag can be days to weeks (Credit Karma, NerdWallet).
3. Utilization guidance, CFPB:
   - "Understand your credit score" (consumerfinance.gov): "Experts advise keeping your use of credit at no more than 30 percent of your total credit limit."
   - CFPB "How to rebuild your credit" handout: some experts advise using no more than 30 percent of your total credit limit, while others say less than 10 percent.
   - CFPB (Ask CFPB / blog): paying off credit cards in full every month is the best way to improve or maintain a good score; you don't need to carry a balance.
   (Search snippets of consumerfinance.gov pages; direct fetch was blocked. Wording should be re-checked by fact-checker.)
4. Top scorers, Experian: "individuals with exceptional FICO Scores typically maintain utilization rates lower than 10%" (Ask Experian, via search snippet).
   Related: Experian page on 800 scores: average utilization 7.7% among consumers with FICO 800; Experian report on 850 scores: about 4% vs 29% for all consumers;
   FICO/CNBC (Dec 2019): top-scoring 25% (above 795) use an average 7% of their limits. Experian also says there is no hard line between good and bad utilization.
5. Surprising fact: paying in full every month can still show 50% utilization on your report if the statement balance was high, because
   the balance is reported before the payment.

## Worked example (hypothetical, labeled as example)
Credit limit $3,000. Charges during the cycle; statement closes with a $1,500 balance; paid in full by the due date (no interest).
Reported utilization = $1,500 / $3,000 = 0.50 = 50%.
If the same $1,500 had been paid down to $250 before the statement closed: $250 / $3,000 = 8.3%.
(Interest is $0 in both cases because the balance is paid by the due date; only the reported utilization differs.)
