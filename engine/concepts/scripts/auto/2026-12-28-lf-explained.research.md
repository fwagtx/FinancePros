## Hook / surprising facts
- NBER announced on December 1, 2008 that a recession had begun in December 2007: about 12 months after it started (NBER BCDC announcement, Dec 1, 2008).
- NBER announced the March 2001 peak on November 26, 2001 (NBER FAQ): about 8 months later.
- NBER announced the February 2020 peak on June 8, 2020 (NBER announcements index): about 4 months later.
- "Two negative GDP quarters" is NOT the official definition. NBER FAQ: it does not use that rule; the 2001 recession did not include two consecutive quarters of real GDP decline.
- 2022 H1: as BEA reported in 2022, real GDP fell at a 1.6% annual rate in Q1 2022 and 0.6% in Q2 2022 (third estimate, Sept 29, 2022). NBER never dated a recession in 2022. (Later annual revisions changed these numbers; a 2024 revision reportedly moved Q2 2022 slightly positive per economist Jeffrey Frankel's blog, not confirmed on bea.gov, so the script says "as first reported in 2022".)
- Unemployment usually keeps rising after a recession officially ends: 2007-09 recession ended June 2009, unemployment peaked 10.0% in October 2009 (BLS). 1990-91 ended March 1991, peak 7.8% June 1992. 2001 ended Nov 2001, peak 6.3% June 2003.

## Definition (NBER)
- NBER FAQ wording: "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and that lasts more than a few months." Shown on a board as a paraphrase/definition, attributed to NBER.
- Three criteria: depth, diffusion, duration; somewhat interchangeable (extreme on one can offset weaker another).
- NBER is a private, nonprofit research organization (not a government agency).
- Dated by NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee, a small group of economists; uses economy-wide measures (jobs, real income, spending, industrial production, GDP); waits for revised data.

- Gaps between recessions are uneven: the expansion before the July 1981 peak lasted 12 months; the one before the March 2001 peak lasted 120 months (NBER table via NMSU Arrowhead).
- Postwar: 11 recessions 1945-2009 averaged about 11 months (NBER table average 11.1 months). Medium-confidence (from memory of NBER table; not re-shown in search) -> script avoids it.

## Unemployment peaks (BLS, monthly, seasonally adjusted)
- 9.0% May 1975 (FRASER/Chicago Fed era sources). Up from 4.6% Oct 1973.
- 10.8% late 1982 (Nov-Dec 1982); BLS: Sept 1982-June 1983 above 10%, peak 10.8%.
- 10.0% October 2009 (BLS Spotlight 2012).
- 14.8% April 2020, highest since data began in 1948 (BLS blog "Looking back on 2020"); first reported 14.7%.
- April 2020 payrolls fell 20.5 million, largest monthly drop on record (BLS TED).
- Dec 2007 to Dec 2009: payrolls down 7.2 million (BLS CES highlights Dec 2009); another 1.2M lost until Feb 2010 trough (BLS TED 2011).

## Causes with history
- Oil shocks: 1973 embargo pushed oil from about $3 to nearly $12 a barrel (multiple sources: Confluence $2.90 -> $11.65 Jan 1974; NBC). 1979 Iranian revolution: about $14 in 1978 to above $30 two years later (NBC citing Lehman).
- Fed tightening: Fed History "Recession of 1981-82": late 1980/early 1981 the Fed let the fed funds rate approach 20 percent; by October 1982 inflation had fallen to 5 percent. Unemployment 10.8% in 1982.
- Bubble bursts: Nasdaq fell about 78% from March 2000 peak (5,048) to October 2002 (~1,114). 2001 recession Mar-Nov 2001.
- Housing/financial crisis: national home prices fell for years after mid-2006 (S&P/Case-Shiller); Lehman Brothers bankruptcy September 2008 (widely reported). S&P 500 fell about 57% from Oct 2007 peak to March 2009 low (666), regained the peak around March 2013 (secondary sources: Visual Capitalist, county plan docs).
- Pandemic: S&P 500 fell about 34% Feb 19 - Mar 23, 2020. Fed cut its target range to 0-0.25% (near zero) in December 2008 and again in March 2020 (Federal Reserve).
- Common mechanism (explanatory, no number): a shock makes people/businesses spend less -> sales fall -> layoffs -> less spending (feedback loop).

## Signals economists watch (attributed, no forecast)
- Yield curve: normally the 10-year Treasury yields more than the 3-month bill; inverted = short above long.
- CNBC/Benzinga (2023): inversion reliably predicted each of the previous eight US recessions since 1970.
- Yield curve: NY Fed model uses spread between 10-year and 3-month Treasury yields to estimate recession odds 12 months ahead. Inversion has preceded recessions since about 1970 (Benzinga/CNBC; not perfect). The 10y-3m spread inverted end of October 2022 and ended December 2024 positive for first time in 27 months (Sentimentrader). NY Fed probability hit 68.2% in April 2023 (Benzinga). As of the latest info found (mid-2026), NBER had dated no recession after it.
- Sahm rule (Claudia Sahm): signals when the 3-month average unemployment rate rises 0.5 percentage point or more above its low of the previous 12 months. Built to trigger automatic stimulus payments, not as a forecast. Triggered with July 2024 data (unemployment 4.3%, released Aug 2024); no recession dated afterwards (as of mid-2026 info).
- Current, dated: unemployment rate 4.2% in September 2026, payrolls +29,000 (BLS Employment Situation, released Oct 2, 2026). Use only as "as of September 2026", no forecast.

## Worked examples (our own math, labeled)
1. Stock drop math: $10,000 falls 57% -> $10,000 x 0.43 = $4,300. To get back to $10,000 needs $10,000 / $4,300 = 2.326 -> a gain of about 133%. A 34% drop: $6,600 left, needs about 52% (10,000/6,600 = 1.515).
2. Savings runway: example person with $9,000 saved and $3,000 a month of essential bills: 9,000 / 3,000 = 3 months of runway. At $18,000 saved: 6 months.
3. Savings interest when rates fall (assumed rates): $10,000 at 4.5% = $450 a year; at 0.5% = $50 a year.
