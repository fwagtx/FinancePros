## Definitions
- Index: a scoreboard of stocks (e.g., the S&P 500, about 500 large US companies, weighted by market value).
- Index fund: a fund that simply buys every stock in an index, in the same proportions, to match it (minus a small fee). No stock picking.
- Active fund: a manager picks stocks to try to beat a benchmark, and charges more for the work.
- Passive = index-tracking funds (index mutual funds + index ETFs).
- Expense ratio: the yearly % of your balance a fund keeps for its costs; taken out of fund returns, not billed.
- ETF: a fund whose shares trade on an exchange all day like a stock.

## History (timeline)
1. 1971: Wells Fargo (William Fouse, John McQuown) set up a $6 million portfolio for the Samsonite pension fund to hold all NYSE stocks, equally weighted; one of the first index funds, for institutions only. Equal weighting needed constant rebalancing (high trading costs); a few years later it switched to the cap-weighted S&P 500. Sources: CFA Institute Enterprising Investor, "A Pillar of Modern Finance Turns 50" (July 1, 2021); Capital Ideas Online, "A history of indexing"; Wikipedia "Index fund"; Dimensional, "1971: The Beginning of a New Way to Invest".
2. Aug 31, 1976: Vanguard's First Index Investment Trust (now the Vanguard 500 Index Fund) began operations. Target: $150 million; raised $11.3 million in its IPO. Bogle called it a "complete flop"; the industry called it "Bogle's Folly" and indexing "un-American." With so little money it could buy only 280 of the 500 stocks at first. Sources: IFA "Fifty Years of Bogle's Folly"; Jason Zweig, "Birth of the Index Mutual Fund: 'Bogle's Folly' Turns 40"; Morningstar "The Index Fund Turns 50"; Axios Aug 31, 2026.
3. Jan 22, 1993: SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust (SPY), the first US-listed ETF, launched (began trading on the American Stock Exchange Jan 29, 1993). Sources: State Street SSGA SPY page; Wikipedia; etf.com. SPY assets about $809 billion as of Oct 1, 2026 (search summary of State Street/ETF data sites; ~$817B late Sept 2026) -> say "more than $800 billion" as of September/October 2026.
4. Buffett bet, Jan 1, 2008 - Dec 31, 2017: Buffett bet that an S&P 500 index fund (he picked Vanguard 500 Index Fund Admiral Shares) would beat a group of hedge funds, net of fees, over 10 years. Ted Seides of Protégé Partners took it and picked five funds-of-funds. Result: index fund +125.8% total; the five funds-of-funds +21.7%, +42.3%, +87.7%, +2.8%, +27.0% (simple average 36.3%). The bet's prize had grown to about $2.2 million; it went to Girls Inc. of Omaha. Sources: Buffett 2017 Berkshire shareholder letter (Feb 2018) as quoted by AEI (Carpe Diem), Long Now Foundation; CNBC Feb 16, 2018; CNN Money Feb 24, 2018; Institutional Investor.
5. Quote (exact): "If a statue is ever erected to honor the person who has done the most for American investors, the hands-down choice should be Jack Bogle." Warren Buffett, 2016 Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letter (Feb 2017). Sources: CNBC Feb 25, 2017; Forbes Feb 25, 2017; Yahoo Finance.

## Scale (dated)
- Passive share of combined US mutual fund + ETF assets (Morningstar data): 3% in 1995, 14% in 2005, 41% in March 2020 (Boston Fed working paper RPA 18-04, "The Shift from Active to Passive Investing", revised 2020, citing Morningstar).
- Dec 31, 2023: passive US funds $13.252 trillion vs active $13.244 trillion: passive passed active for the first time (Morningstar data; CNBC Jan 18, 2024; Morningstar "It's Official: Passive Funds Overtake Active Funds"). = 50.0%.
- Year-end 2024: passive about 53% of US fund assets; year-end 2025: over 55% (Morningstar, "8 Charts on US Fund Flows in 2025", Jan 2026; 2025 US Fund Flows report). Oct 2025: passive ~$19.1T vs active ~$16.2T (Morningstar, per search summary; secondary).

## Performance
- SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2025 (S&P Dow Jones Indices, March 2026): 79% of active large-cap US funds trailed the S&P 500 in 2025 (65% in 2024), the fourth-worst year in SPIVA's 25-year history. (Already used as an annual chart in the 11-26 short; here use long horizons.)
- Long horizons to Dec 31, 2025: "at least 80% of equity funds underperformed their respective benchmarks over the 10-year period" (S&P DJI summary text); large-cap 10-year about 84-86%, 15-year about 89.5-90% (search summaries disagree on decimals: 85.59%/89.93%; 89.50%). WEAK on exact decimals -> script says "more than 8 in 10 over 10 years" and "about 9 in 10 over 15 years".
- SPIVA U.S. Mid-Year 2026 (Sept 2026): 67% of large-cap funds trailed in H1 2026 (better than 2025). Long-horizon still ~83% (10y) / ~90% (15y) per search summary (WEAK decimals).
- Morningstar US Active/Passive Barometer mid-year 2026: just over 40% of active funds survived and beat their passive peers over the year to June 2026 (up 7 points), but only about 25% did over 10 years (Morningstar; InvestmentNews "Active funds gained ground in 2026, but 75% still trail passive over 10 years").
- Fair caveats: index funds never beat their index (they match it minus fees); some active funds do win; SPIVA counts closed/merged funds as losers.

## Debates (neutral)
- Concentration: the Big Three (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) average combined stake in S&P 500 companies rose from 5.2% in 1998 and 13.5% in 2008 to 20.5% in 2017; they cast about 25% of votes at S&P 500 meetings (Bebchuk & Hirst, "The Specter of the Giant Three," Boston Univ. Law Review 2019 / NBER w25914). Later update: about 22% by 2023 (search summary; WEAK). Fichtner, Heemskerk & Garcia-Bernardo (2017): together the largest shareholder in about 88% of S&P 500 firms (2015 data).
- Common ownership: Azar, Schmalz & Tecu (Journal of Finance, 2018) linked common ownership of rival airlines to ticket prices about 3-7% higher (search summary quotes 3-11%; use "a few percent" - no exact number on screen). Dennis, Gerardi & Schenone (Journal of Finance, 2022) found no anticompetitive effect after reanalysis. Unsettled.
- "Too big?" counterpoint: index domestic equity mutual funds and ETFs held 18% of the value of US stocks at year-end 2024 (ICI 2025 Fact Book). So passive is over half of fund assets, but under a fifth of the stock market. (Single search; moderate.)
- Response: pass-through voting. BlackRock Voting Choice available for $3.96 trillion of its $8.79 trillion index equity assets as of Dec 2025 (BlackRock 2026 Global Voting Spotlight); Vanguard expanded Investor Choice in 2025 to 12 funds, nearly $1 trillion, ~10 million investors (Sustainable Investing June 16, 2025; Vanguard).

## Worked example (exact math; hypothetical)
Assumptions: $10,000 lump sum, 30 years, 7% gross annual return (assumption, not a prediction), fee subtracted from return each year (net = 7% - fee), fees = ICI 2025 averages.
- Index-like fee 0.05%: 10,000 x 1.0695^30 = $75,062.61
- Active-like fee 0.64%: 10,000 x 1.0636^30 = $63,584.29
- Gap: $11,478.32 (about 15% of the low-fee result)
- No fee: 10,000 x 1.07^30 = $76,122.55. So 0.05% costs $1,059.94 over 30 years; 0.64% costs $12,538.26.
- Year one fee on $10,000: $5 vs $64.
Buffett bet in dollars: $100 in the index fund -> $225.80; $100 at the funds' average 36.3% -> $136.30.

## Surprising fact
Wall Street mocked the first index mutual fund as "Bogle's Folly" in 1976 (it raised $11.3M of a $150M goal and could buy only 280 stocks). Fifty years later the fund holds more than $1.6 trillion, and passive funds hold more than half of all US fund assets (over 55% at end-2025).
