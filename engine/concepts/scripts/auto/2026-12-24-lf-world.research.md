# Research brief: Dollarization: Why Ecuador and Panama Use the U.S. Dollar
Slot 2026-12-24-lf-world (posts Thursday, December 24, 2026). Researched October 25, 2026.
Evergreen: every number carries its year/source. No "this week".

Dollarized countries cannot set their own rates, so they import Fed policy. (Federal Reserve FOMC statement, Sept 16, 2026; CNBC Sept 16, 2026 — confirmed in earlier FinancePros briefs.) - Ecuador's dollarization passed its 25th anniversary in 2025 (announced Jan 9, 2000).

## The surprising fact
- Ecuador gave up its own currency in 2000 at 25,000 sucres per dollar; and Panama has used the U.S. dollar since 1904 — more than 120 years, with no central bank printing its own paper money. In 1941 Panama did print its own balboa banknotes; they lasted about a week (the "seven-day" notes) before a new government withdrew and burned them.

## Panama (since 1904)
- 1904: Monetary agreement with the U.S. made the U.S. gold dollar legal tender in Panama at par; the national unit, the balboa, equals one U.S. dollar (State Dept, FRUS historical documents; Wikipedia "Panamanian balboa").
- The balboa exists as coins only, at 1:1 with the dollar; U.S. dollar bills are the paper money (Wikipedia; multiple).
- No conventional central bank; Banco Nacional de Panamá is a state bank; banks supervised by a banking regulator (Wikipedia; Mises Institute). 
- Oct 2, 1941: President Arnulfo Arias' Banco Central de Emisión issued 1, 5, 10, 20 balboa notes; about a week later Arias was overthrown (Oct 9, 1941), the new government closed the bank, withdrew the notes and burned unissued stock. Collectors call them "seven-day notes" (Wikipedia; Banknote World; Stack's Bowers / CoinWorld).

## Ecuador (1998-2000 crisis)
- 1998: exchange band devalued twice; sucre fell 54% in 1998 (IMF). Feb 1999: central bank abandoned crawling peg and floated the sucre (US gov document / IMF).
- March 8-12, 1999: bank holiday ("feriado bancario"), followed by a freeze of deposits that lasted months (Revista Uruguaya de Historia Económica; El Comercio).
- Sucre went from about 7,000 per dollar in January 1999 to nearly 30,000 by end-1999 at its worst (Christian Science Monitor, Aug 30, 2000); lost 67% in 1999 and a further 17% in the first week of 2000, ending at 25,000 on Jan 7, 2000 (Wikipedia "Ecuadorian sucre"; IMF).
- Jan 9, 2000: President Jamil Mahuad announced dollarization. Jan 21, 2000: Mahuad ousted; VP Gustavo Noboa took office and continued dollarization (IPS; ABC News).
- Feb 29, 2000: Congress passed the "Ley Trolebús" (Law for the Economic Transformation of Ecuador); became law March 13, 2000 (Wikipedia; Springer 2024 article). Rate fixed at 25,000 sucres = US$1. Sucre notes exchangeable at the central bank until about end of March 2001 (sources differ: Mar 30/31, 2001).
- Emigration: INEC-cited figure of about 900,000 Ecuadorians emigrating 1999-2000 (Extra.ec citing INEC) — WEAKLY SOURCED; in script say "hundreds of thousands" / "one of the largest emigration waves in its history" (El Comercio).
- Ecuador mints its own centavo coins (since 2000), used alongside U.S. coins and bills; it issues no banknotes (Wikipedia "Ecuadorian centavo coins").

## Worked example (hypothetical, labeled)
- Example savings of 10,000,000 sucres.
- January 1999, about 7,000 sucres per dollar: 10,000,000 / 7,000 = 1,428.57 -> about $1,429.
- Conversion rate 25,000 per dollar (2000): 10,000,000 / 25,000 = $400.
- Loss in dollar terms: 1 - 400/1,428.57 = 0.72 -> about 72%.
- Second example: price in 2000 inflation 96.1%: something costing $1.00-equivalent at start of 2000 cost about $1.96 a year later (1 x 1.961 = 1.961).

## El Salvador
- Jan 1, 2001: Monetary Integration Law (President Francisco Flores) — dollar circulates freely, fixed rate 8.75 colones per dollar; by end-2001 mostly only dollars circulated (Wikipedia "Salvadoran colón"; Tico Times; IPS).
- Bitcoin Law: passed June 8/9, 2021; took effect Sept 7, 2021 — first country to make bitcoin legal tender; businesses generally had to accept it (Wikipedia "Bitcoin Law"; Nasdaq; IBA).
- Dec 2024: $1.4 billion IMF loan agreement (staff-level). Jan 29, 2025: Legislative Assembly amended the law 55-2: businesses no longer obligated to accept bitcoin; taxes can't be paid in bitcoin; bitcoin no longer described as currency (Wikipedia; Cointelegraph; LatamFDI). Neutral wording: "made bitcoin voluntary"; the U.S. dollar remains the currency.

## Others
- Zimbabwe: abandoned its own dollar in April 2009 after hyperinflation (official 231 million percent in 2008); used a basket incl. US dollar, rand, pula etc. ("multicurrency"); brought back a Zimbabwe dollar in June 2019; launched ZiG (gold-backed) April 5, 2024; US dollar still widely used; Feb 2026 central bank dropped its 2030 deadline for ending the multicurrency system (Wikipedia; Nehanda Radio Feb 27, 2026; Graphic.com.gh).
- Timor-Leste: U.S. dollar since 2000 under UN administration; own centavo coins since 2003 (Wikipedia).
- Micronesia, Marshall Islands, Palau: use U.S. dollar; tied to Compacts of Free Association (Stripe; Wise).
- Also: British Virgin Islands, Turks and Caicos, Bonaire/Sint Eustatius/Saba (since Jan 1, 2011), and U.S. territories.
- Contrast: Hong Kong is a PEG (currency board since Oct 17, 1983, band HK$7.75-7.85 per US$1) — keeps its own currency; dollarization drops your own currency entirely. (HKMA; prior Dec 10 brief.)

## Argentina
- Milei campaigned in 2023 on dollarization / closing the central bank. Feb 2024 he said dollarization is the final step of a long process, not a short-term goal (Buenos Aires Times). 2025: crawling band under $20 billion IMF program; from Jan 1, 2026 band limits adjusted monthly by inflation (Dec 2025 reports). As of the latest available reporting (2026), Argentina still uses the peso; dollarization not adopted. (PIIE 2026; Funds Society; BA Times.) Neutral.

## Pros and cons (standard economics; IMF, textbook)
Pros: inflation falls to near U.S. levels (Ecuador from 96.1% in 2000 to 2.7% by 2004); no currency crash risk; prices and contracts in dollars; often lower interest rates/credibility; easier long-term loans.
Cons: no lender of last resort (cannot print dollars to rescue banks); cannot set interest rates — imports Fed decisions (e.g. Sept 2026 hike); cannot devalue to regain competitiveness when the dollar is strong; lost seigniorage (the profit from issuing money goes to the U.S.); must earn dollars through exports, oil, remittances, borrowing; hard to exit.

## Wallet angle
- Remittances: Ecuador received a record $7.729 billion in remittances in 2025, up 18.2% from $6.54 billion in 2024; about 78% ($6.01 billion) from the U.S.; ~22.3 million transfers (Banco Central del Ecuador via El Comercio, El Mercurio, Portafolio). Dollars sent arrive as dollars — no currency conversion.
- Savings: dollar savings can't be wiped out by devaluation; but bank freezes still possible.
- U.S. travelers: no currency exchange in Ecuador, Panama, El Salvador.
