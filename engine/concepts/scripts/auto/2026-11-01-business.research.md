# Research brief: How bail bond companies make money (posts Nov 1, 2026)

Neutral framing: explain the money mechanics; no position on bail policy.

## Model
- Bail = money a court holds to secure a defendant's return. Paid in full to the court, it is returned (minus possible fees) if the defendant appears.
- Commercial bail agent (bondsman) posts a surety bond for the full amount and charges a nonrefundable premium, commonly about 10% of the bail (rates are state-regulated and vary).
- The bond is typically backed by a surety insurance company, which takes about 10% of the premium, i.e., about 1% of the bond's face amount (Texas Senate bill analysis, 2013; ACLU / Prison Policy Initiative reports).
- Agents usually require collateral (e.g., car or home title) and co-signers; if the defendant misses court and the bond is forfeited, the agent (then insurer) owes the full amount, so agents track down no-shows.
- Commercial bail is essentially limited to the US and the Philippines (PolitiFact, Oct 2018 fact check). Several states (e.g., Illinois, Kentucky, Oregon, Wisconsin) don't allow commercial bail bonds.

## Worked example
- Bail $20,000 -> premium 10% = $2,000 (nonrefundable, even if charges are dropped).
- Insurer about 10% of premium = $200 (1% of $20,000). Agent keeps about $1,800.
- If defendant misses court: agent can owe $20,000 (recovered from collateral/co-signers where possible).
- Paying $20,000 cash to the court instead: returned if you appear.

## Data (BJS, felony defendants, 75 largest US counties)
- Share of pretrial releases by commercial surety bond: 24% in 1990-1994 vs 42% in 2002-2004; surety bond passed release on recognizance as the most common release type in 1998 (Cohen & Reaves, BJS Special Report NCJ 214994, Nov 2007).
