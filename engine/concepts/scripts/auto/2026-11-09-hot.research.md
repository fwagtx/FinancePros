# Research brief: What happens if your car gets repossessed? (posts Nov 9, 2026)

## Process (CFPB)
- After default (e.g., missed payments, per contract), in many states a lender can repossess without a court order and often without advance notice; repossession agents can't "breach the peace" (state law varies).
- The vehicle is usually sold, often at auction; the sale must be commercially reasonable.
- If sale proceeds don't cover the loan payoff plus repossession, storage and sale costs, the borrower owes a deficiency balance (an unsecured debt; may go to collections or lawsuit).
- A repossession can remain on credit reports for up to 7 years (FCRA).
- CFPB suggests contacting the lender early if you can't pay; lenders may offer options such as deferrals or modifications.

## Numbers
- CFPB report on repossession (Jan 2025): average deficiency balance among consumers with one: $10,747 (Dec 2019), $7,971 (Dec 2021), $11,340 (Dec 2022).
- Cox Automotive: ~1.73 million vehicles repossessed in 2024, +16% vs 2023, +43% vs 2022, most since 2009.
- Fitch: subprime auto ABS 60+ day delinquency record 6.90% in Jan 2026 (highest since 1994 index start); 5.80% in June 2026.

## Worked example (hypothetical)
Owe $20,000; car sells at auction for $12,000; repo and sale fees $1,500: deficiency = 20,000 - 12,000 + 1,500 = $9,500, and no car.
