# Research brief: Money dysmorphia (feeling broke when you're not)
Slot: 2026-11-08-explainer. Researched October 1, 2026. Posts November 8, 2026. Evergreen; every figure dated.

## Definition / framing
- "Money dysmorphia" = a popular, informal term for a distorted view of your own finances: feeling broke, behind or insecure when your numbers say otherwise. It borrows from "body dysmorphia". It is NOT a clinical diagnosis (body dysmorphic disorder is a recognized condition; money dysmorphia is not). Sources: Bankrate "What Is Money Dysmorphia"; TODAY.com; Boldin.
- Term was popularized by an Intuit Credit Karma survey released in early 2024 (Fortune 1/29/2024, CNBC 3/13/2024, Bloomberg Opinion 2/6/2024 covered it).
- Script must describe, not diagnose or give mental-health advice.

## Key numbers
### Intuit Credit Karma (survey by Qualtrics), released early 2024
- Methodology: online survey of 1,006 U.S. adults 18+, conducted Dec 18-26, 2023, by Qualtrics for Intuit Credit Karma.
- 29% of Americans say they experience money dysmorphia.
- 43% of Gen Z and 41% of millennials.
- 48% of Gen Z and 59% of millennials say they feel behind financially.
- 44% of Gen Z / 46% of millennials "obsessed with the idea of being rich".
- 37% of respondents with money dysmorphia report > $10,000 in savings; 23% > $30,000. (Credit Karma's comparison to a "$5,300 median" not independently confirmed; NOT used.)
Sources: creditkarma.com/about/commentary (Gen Z and millennials are obsessed...); CNBC 3/13/2024; Fortune 1/29/2024.

### Northwestern Mutual Planning & Progress Study (The Harris Poll)
- 2024 study: 4,588 U.S. adults 18+, online Jan 3-17, 2024. Average net worth Americans say they need to feel "financially comfortable": $778,000 (down from $1 million peak the prior year). Net worth needed "to be considered wealthy": $2.5 million, up from $2.2 million two years earlier (2022). (No newer dollar figure for "wealthy" found in 2025 or 2026 releases; label as 2024.)
- 2025 study: 4,626 U.S. adults 18+, online Jan 2-19, 2025. Millionaire findings released Nov 4, 2025: only 36% of Americans with $1 million+ in investable assets consider themselves "wealthy" (so 64% don't). 49% of millionaires say their financial planning needs improvement.
- 2024 comparison: 32% of millionaires considered themselves wealthy (Sept 4, 2024 release).
- 2026 study (4,375 adults, Jan 5-21, 2026, released March 2026): "magic number" to retire comfortably $1.46 million (context only).
Sources: news.northwesternmutual.com releases 2024-09-04, 2025-11-04, 2026-03-09, 2026-04-01; 401kspecialistmag "Feeling 'Financially Comfortable' Takes Less in 2024"; Quartz; InvestmentNews.

### Federal Reserve SHED (Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking)
- "Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025", published May 2026 (press release May 13, 2026). Survey of nearly 13,000 adults, fielded Oct 17-28, 2025, weighted to represent U.S. adults 18+.
- 73% of adults were "doing okay" (39%) or "living comfortably" (34%) financially, late 2025. Held at or near 73% since 2022.

## Worked example (exact math), NM 2024 averages
- Wealthy minus comfortable: $2,500,000 - $778,000 = $1,722,000 (about $1.72 million).
- Ratio: $2,500,000 / $778,000 = 3.21 (more than three times).
- "Wealthy" bar moved: $2.2M (2022) -> $2.5M (2024) = +$300,000.
- Millionaires: 100% - 36% = 64% of $1M+ investors don't call themselves wealthy (2025 study).

## Surprising fact
Nearly two in three U.S. millionaires (64%, NM 2025) don't consider themselves wealthy.
