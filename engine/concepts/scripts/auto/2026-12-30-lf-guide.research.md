# Research brief: How to build an emergency fund from nothing (lf-guide, posts Wed Dec 30, 2026)

Evergreen. No "today/this week". Every number carries its date or period.
Note: research done with web search only (direct fetches of federalreserve.gov, bankrate.com, fdic.gov, consumerfinance.gov, FRED were blocked in this session); figures below are confirmed by search snippets of the official page plus at least one secondary source unless marked WEAK.

## Hook / surprising fact
- Federal Reserve SHED, "Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025" (released May 13, 2026; survey of ~13,000 adults, October 2025): 63% of adults would cover a $400 emergency expense completely with cash or its equivalent, unchanged from 2024. So about 37% (over a third) would not: they would borrow, sell something, or could not pay.
- "Cash or its equivalent" in the SHED includes cash, money in checking/savings, or a credit card paid in full at the next statement. The question asks HOW people would pay, not whether they have $400 saved (Cato Institute critique; worth one honest line).
- Surprise: the share peaked at 68% in 2021 (pandemic-era aid/savings) and fell back to 63% by 2022 and stayed there through 2025.

## Chartable series (Fed SHED, share of adults who would cover a $400 emergency with cash or equivalent)
Evenly spaced every 4 years (all values confirmed via Fed SHED data table/reports):
- 2013: 50%
- 2017: 59%
- 2021: 68% (peak)
- 2025: 63%
(Also confirmed: 2019 63%, 2023 63%, 2024 63%. 2015 value not confirmed (one source implies 54%); do not use.)

## Other emergency-savings data
- Fed SHED 2025 (May 2026): 55% of adults had set aside emergency or rainy-day savings that would cover three months of expenses; unchanged from 2024, down from a high of 59% in 2021.
- Bankrate 2026 Annual Emergency Savings Report (published Feb 4, 2026; survey December 2025): 24% (nearly 1 in 4) of Americans have no emergency savings; 46% have enough to cover three months of expenses (19% three to five months + 27% six months or more); 30% have some savings but less than three months. WEAK-ish: confirmed only via search snippets/secondary coverage (NRMLA Feb 6, 2026 summary); the same breakdown also appeared in Bankrate's mid-2025 survey, so prefer the Fed numbers on screen and use "nearly 1 in 4 with no emergency savings" only with "Bankrate, 2026 report".
- Bankrate January 2026 Financial Security release: 54% say they're saving less for unexpected expenses because of inflation. (Not used.)

## Guidance and definitions
- CFPB ("Start Small, Save Up"; "An essential guide to building an emergency fund", April 2021): an emergency fund is cash set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies (car repair, medical bill, job loss). CFPB suggests sizing the goal from the unexpected expenses you've actually had and what they cost, and starting small; even small amounts of savings help. (Wording confirmed via secondary summaries only.)
- 3 to 6 months of ESSENTIAL expenses: common rule of thumb in many financial guides (not a law; attribute as "a common rule of thumb"). Essentials = rent/mortgage, utilities, groceries, insurance, transportation, minimum debt payments.
- Starter goal of $500 or $1,000: common practice in financial guides; frame as an option. (Some secondary sources attribute a $1,000 starter goal to CFPB; NOT confirmed on CFPB site, so do not attribute.)
- What counts as an emergency: unexpected, necessary, urgent (job loss, car repair needed to get to work, medical or dental bill, urgent home repair, emergency travel). Not emergencies: planned/predictable costs (holidays, annual insurance premium, vacations, sales) - those are "sinking fund" goals.

## Where to keep it (rules)
- FDIC: standard deposit insurance coverage limit is $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, per ownership category. Covers checking, savings, CDs, money market deposit accounts. Does NOT cover stocks, bonds, mutual funds, crypto. (FDIC FAQ.)
- NCUA: credit union share insurance, $250,000 per share owner, per insured credit union, per ownership category; covers savings, checking, share certificates, money market shares; backed by full faith and credit of the US (NCUA / MyCreditUnion.gov).
- FDIC national average savings rate (National Rates and Rate Caps, via FRED series SNDR): 0.38% as of July 2026 (also cited for August 2026); 0.39% in Feb-Mar 2026. Some secondary sites report 0.37% for the Sept 21, 2026 release (unconfirmed, do not use).
- Online/high-yield accounts can pay many times the average; we have shorts on that ("Why online banks can pay 10x the average savings rate", Oct 25; "Why one bank pays $1 and another pays $400", Nov 16). Mention briefly only; no specific product or rate.
- Trade-offs: savings/money market deposit account = insured, instant access, variable rate. CDs = insured but early-withdrawal penalty. Stocks = can fall right when you need the money; not insured.

## At work (SECURE 2.0)
- Pension-linked emergency savings accounts (PLESAs), created by SECURE 2.0, allowed from Jan 1, 2024; employer's choice to offer (optional). Roth (after-tax) employee contributions; withdrawals at least once a month; first four withdrawals per plan year free of fees; can be limited to non-highly-compensated employees. Contributions-attributable balance capped at $2,500 indexed: $2,600 for 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67, Nov 2025; confirmed by HRMorning, NAIFA, DLA Piper). 2027 figure not found yet (IRS usually announces in Oct/Nov) - say "$2,600 in 2026".
- SECURE 2.0 emergency personal expense distribution (from Jan 1, 2024; IRS Notice 2024-55, June 20, 2024): one per calendar year, up to $1,000 (not indexed), exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal penalty (still taxable); generally no another one for the next three calendar years unless repaid or replaced. Frame as a last resort that shrinks retirement savings.

## Cost of NOT having one
- Average credit card interest rate on accounts assessed interest: 22.15% in Q2 2026 (Fed G.19, via FRED TERMCBCCINTNS); a tracker reports 22.36% for August 2026 (released Oct 7, 2026). Say "about 22% in 2026, Fed data".

## Worked examples (OUR MATH)
1. $25 a week: 20 weeks = $500; 40 weeks = $1,000; 52 weeks = $1,300.
2. $50 a week: 20 weeks = $1,000; 52 weeks = $2,600.
3. Target, hypothetical essentials of $2,500 a month: 3 months = $7,500; 6 months = $15,000. At $50 a week, $7,500 takes 150 weeks (about 2.9 years) - so add windfalls and raises.
4. Interest on $1,000 for a year at the 0.38% national average (July 2026): $3.80. (No specific high-yield rate stated.)
5. $1,000 emergency on a credit card at 22% APR: about $18.33 of interest per month (1,000 x 0.22 / 12), roughly $220 if carried a whole year (simple estimate, ignores compounding and payments).
6. Round-up idea: spare change; illustrative only, no math claim.
