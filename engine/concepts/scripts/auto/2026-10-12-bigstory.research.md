## The story: losing a job is rare, but getting rehired is slow, and benefits often run out first

### Facts (each with source and date)
- **"Nearly four in ten people exhaust their unemployment benefits before they land a new job."** NPR, Scott Horsley, "It's taking longer to find a new job after losing one," published Oct 11, 2026 (carried by many NPR member stations, e.g. KPBS, WGCU, TPR).
  - Cross-check: US Dept. of Labor ETA UI Data Dashboard, regular state programs, exhaustion rate (avg monthly exhaustions / avg monthly first payments, numerator lagged 26 weeks): 39.80 for 12 months ending 03/31/2026; 36.24 for 12 months ending 07/31/2026; a search snippet showed 39.27 for 12 months ending 08/31/2026 (not confirmed; snapshots differ). ### Context / history
- "Low-hire, low-fire" labor market: weekly jobless claims near the lowest since the late 1960s, yet hiring is slow (29,000 jobs in September), so people who do lose work stay unemployed longer.
- Regular state UI typically lasts up to 26 weeks in most states; some states (North Carolina, Florida, others) tie the maximum to the state unemployment rate and pay much less. Extended Benefits only trigger in periods of high unemployment.

### What it means for a normal person's money
- If a layoff happens, the benefit clock (12 to 26 weeks depending on state) can be shorter than the job search; about 27% of the unemployed have been out 27+ weeks, longer than most states pay.
- Example math (labeled example): 12 weeks x the $492 national average weekly benefit = $5,904 in total, and then payments stop (actual amounts depend on state caps; NC caps at $350/week).
- Things people check when planning for a layoff: their state's maximum weeks, the state's weekly benefit cap, and how many months of expenses their savings cover. (Education, not advice.)
