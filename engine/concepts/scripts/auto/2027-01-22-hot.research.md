# 2027-01-22-hot research brief: Money myth, "no interest if paid in full" = 0% APR?

Made Nov 23, 2026 for posting Fri Jan 22, 2027. Evergreen. No news peg needed (the CFPB findings are older but still describe how the products work).
Research note: Firecrawl was out of credits and consumerfinance.gov could not be fetched directly from this session (host not allowed), so CFPB figures were confirmed via web search result summaries of CFPB pages and of reports citing them. Items marked [secondary] should be re-checked against the CFPB PDF by the fact-checker.

## Core definitions (CFPB consumer blog, "How to understand special promotional financing offers on credit cards"; CFPB June 2017 newsroom release to retail card companies)
- Deferred interest ("No interest if paid in full in 12 months"): interest builds in the background from the purchase date. If ANY of the promo balance is left when the promo ends (or the promo ends early, e.g. after a late payment, per the card terms), the cardholder is charged all the interest that built up since the purchase date.
- 0% intro APR ("0% intro APR for 12 months"): no interest builds during the promo. When it ends, interest applies only to the balance still left, going forward.
- The CFPB in 2017 encouraged retail card companies to consider true 0% promotions instead, where consumers are not assessed interest retroactively.
- Deferred-interest cards usually carry a high standard APR (see store-card APRs below).

## Key numbers (with dates)
- CFPB, The Consumer Credit Card Market report (December 2015), looking at deferred-interest promotions 2009-2013 [secondary, via NCLC comments and law-firm summaries]:
  - Among consumers with subprime scores (under 620), more than 40% did not pay off the promo balance in time and were charged lump-sum retroactive interest; about 50% for deep subprime.
  - Prime / superprime consumers paid off in time at rates around or above 80-90%.
  - Roughly three-quarters of deferred-interest balances overall were paid in full within the promo period (so about 1 in 4 were not) [secondary].
  - Almost one-third of those who missed paid off principal plus accrued interest within two billing cycles after the promo ended.
  - Earlier CFPB study (October 2013 CARD Act report): 43% of subprime vs 12% of superprime consumers were charged retroactive deferred interest [secondary, NCLC].
- Store-card (private label) APR: CFPB Consumer Credit Card Market Report (December 2025 edition): average APR 31.3% for private label cards in 2024, versus 25.2% for general purpose cards; highest since at least 2015.
- CFPB Issue Spotlight "The High Cost of Retail Credit Cards" (Dec 2024): average APR on new cards from top retailers 32.66% (as of Dec 2024); 90% of retail cards had a max APR above 30% vs 38% of general purpose cards; 2022 averages 27.7% private label vs 22.7% general purpose.

## Chartable data series
- Average private label (store) card APR, CFPB: 2022 = 27.7%, 2024 = 31.3%. (Only 2 confirmed points; general purpose: mid-2020 low 18.8%, 2022 22.7%, 2024 25.2%.) Not enough evenly spaced confirmed points for a chart, so the script does not use a chart.

## Worked example (OUR MATH, illustrative, no product named)
Assumptions: $1,200 purchase on a 12-month deferred-interest promo, standard APR 29.99% (close to the 2024 store-card average of 31.3%). Payments of $100 at the end of each of months 1-11, nothing in month 12, so $100 is left when the promo ends. Simple (non-compounding) monthly interest on each month's starting balance.
- Monthly rate = 29.99% / 12 = 2.4992%.
- Month-start balances: 1,200, 1,100, 1,000, 900, 800, 700, 600, 500, 400, 300, 200, 100. Sum = $7,800.
- Deferred interest charged at promo end = 7,800 x 0.024992 = $194.94 (about $195).
- So a $100 leftover triggers about $195 of back interest: almost double (1.95x) the amount still owed. Total cost $1,200 + $194.94 = $1,394.94.
- True 0% intro APR card, same payments: $0 interest during the 12 months. Afterward interest applies only to the $100 left: 100 x 0.024992 = about $2.50 per month at 29.99%.
- Method caveat: real issuers use average daily balance and may compound; actual figures vary. This is labeled "our math".

## The surprising fact
Missing the deadline by even $1 can trigger interest back to the purchase date on the whole balance you carried, not just the leftover.
