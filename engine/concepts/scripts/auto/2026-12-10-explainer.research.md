# Research brief: Roth conversions, paying tax now to skip it later
Slot: 2026-12-10-explainer (posts Thu Dec 10, 2026). Researched Sun Oct 11, 2026. Evergreen: every figure dated.
Note: irs.gov could not be fetched directly from this environment (proxy/DNS block); IRS wording below is confirmed through search results quoting IRS pages, cross-checked with reputable secondary sources.

## Core rules (current as of Oct 2026)
1. No income limit on conversions since 2010. The $100,000 modified AGI cap on conversions was eliminated for tax years beginning in 2010 (TIPRA 2005). IRS Topic No. 309: "regardless of the amount of your adjusted gross income, you may be able to convert amounts from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA." (Income limits still apply to direct Roth contributions, not conversions.)
2. Converted amount is taxed as ordinary income in the year of the conversion: you include in gross income the converted amount other than any after-tax basis (pro-rata rule if you have both pre-tax and after-tax IRA money). Reported on Form 8606. Sources: IRS Topic 309; Pub 590-A; Form 8606 instructions (via secondary quotes).
3. Deadline: a conversion counts for the calendar year in which it is completed, so it must be done by Dec 31 (Dec 31, 2026 for tax year 2026). There is no April extension like there is for contributions (contributions for a year can be made until the filing deadline, generally April 15). Sources: Form 8606 instructions / Pub 590-A as cited by q3adv.com, irahelp.com; NAPA-net 2012 example.
4. No recharacterization (undo) of conversions since TCJA: IRS FAQ: "a Roth IRA conversion made on or after January 1, 2018, cannot be recharacterized." (TCJA, effective tax years after Dec 31, 2017; permanent.) Regular contributions can still be recharacterized. Sources: IRS IRA FAQs on recharacterization (quoted by Vanguard, Morgan Lewis, CPA Practice Advisor, Ascensus).
5. Five-year rule per conversion: if you are under 59 1/2 and withdraw a converted amount within 5 years, the taxable part of that conversion can face the 10% additional tax. "The 5-year period ... is separately determined for each conversion." The clock starts Jan 1 of the conversion's tax year. Exceptions: age 59 1/2 or another 72(t) exception. Source: IRS Pub 590-B (quoted by Marotta, early-retirement.org, Empower).
6. Qualified Roth withdrawals later are tax-free (age 59 1/2+ and 5-year rule for the Roth met). Trade-off: you pay tax now at today's rate to avoid tax later at the future rate.

## 2026 federal brackets (IRS IR-2025-103, Oct 9, 2025; Rev. Proc. 2025-32)
Rates unchanged: 10, 12, 22, 24, 32, 35, 37%.
- Single: 12% over $12,400 to $50,400; 22% over $50,400 to $105,700; 37% over $640,600.
- Married filing jointly: 12% over $24,800 to $100,800; 22% over $100,800 to $211,400; 37% over $768,700.
- Standard deduction 2026: $16,100 single, $32,200 joint (2025: $15,750 / $31,500, per OBBBA).
Sources: IRS newsroom IR-2025-103; KPMG TaxNewsFlash on Rev. Proc. 2025-32; CPA Practice Advisor; Accounting Today.

## Worked example (EXAMPLE, stated assumptions)
Assumptions: tax year 2026, single filer, the $10,000 converted is all pre-tax (no basis), federal income tax only (no state tax), whole $10,000 stays inside one bracket.
- Person A: taxable income $40,000 before conversion -> after = $50,000, still within 12% (top $50,400). Tax on conversion = $10,000 x 12% = $1,200.
- Person B: taxable income $70,000 -> after = $80,000, still within 22% ($50,400-$105,700). Tax = $10,000 x 22% = $2,200.
- Difference: $1,000 more for the same conversion. The math of the trade-off: if the money would later be withdrawn at a higher rate than today, paying now costs less; if at a lower rate, paying now costs more. (Neutral; no recommendation.)

## Surprising fact / data
- IRS SOI Bulletin, Fall 2013 ("Individual Retirement Arrangements, 2010", Bryant & Gober): Roth IRA conversions "increased significantly in 2010, rising over 800 percent, to $64.8 billion," surpassing Roth contributions for the first time, mainly because conversions opened to all income levels. About 869,400 taxpayers converted (avg ~$74,534) — count from secondary sources citing the bulletin.
- Chartable series: a year-by-year series (2007 $2.3B, 2008 $3.7B, 2009 $6.8B, 2010 $64.8B, 2011 $11.3B, 2012 $18.1B, 2013 $7.5B, 2014 $8.3B) appears only on a secondary site (q3adv.com / rothirahub.com) and could NOT be confirmed against an IRS table, so it is NOT used in the script. Only the official 2010 figure ($64.8B, up over 800%) is used.
- 2011: TIGTA reported ~400,000 taxpayers converted over $10B (not used).
