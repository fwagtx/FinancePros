# Research brief: Belt and Road: how China lends to the world

## Timely angle (not "this week" news; use as dated context)
- Record first half for Belt and Road "engagement" in 2026: about $49.8B of investment plus $76.5B of construction contracts in H1 2026, the highest for any first six months since 2013 (Nedopil, Griffith Asia Institute / Green Finance & Development Center, "China BRI Investment Report 2026 H1", late July 2026; University of Queensland release; SCMP). Our math: 49.8 + 76.5 = $126.3B.
  - Energy was the largest sector (~$36.3B); renewables 56% of energy engagement (same report).
  - Private firms' share of engagement rose from 12.5% (2020) to 47.7% (H1 2026).
- Contrast: Chinese state LENDING has shrunk. Belt and Road now looks less like a bank and more like an investor/builder, while borrowers repay old loans ("from lender to debt collector", Lowy Institute, May 2025).

## The surprising fact (hook)
- AidData (William & Mary), "Chasing China: Learning to Play by Beijing's Global Lending Rules" (November 18, 2025): China's official sector committed about $2.2 trillion in loans and grants, 2000-2023, to 217 countries and territories (33,580 projects and activities; 1,193 Chinese state donors/lenders).
- The single largest recipient: the United States, nearly $202B (~$201.83B) across ~2,500 projects and activities, mostly to companies and projects, not to the US government (Reuters, AP, NBC, Fortune coverage, Nov 18, 2025). EU about $161B; UK about $60B.
- Nearly $1 trillion (~10,000 projects) went to 72 high-income countries. Only about 20% of the portfolio is infrastructure in developing countries (AidData via coverage). Share of portfolio going to upper-middle and high-income countries rose sharply (AidData; exact figures UNCERTAIN, not used).
- AidData: a large and growing share of China's overseas lending is "going dark" (less transparent). China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs response: its overseas financing "adhered to international practices, market principles and the principle of debt sustainability."
- Hook line (true per AidData): "China's state lenders sent more money to America than anywhere else." (Loans and grants, 2000-2023.)

## What the Belt and Road is
- Proposed by Xi Jinping in autumn 2013: the "Silk Road Economic Belt" (land) at Nazarbayev University, Astana, Kazakhstan, September 7, 2013; the "21st Century Maritime Silk Road" (sea) to Indonesia's parliament, October 2013 (Belt and Road Portal; China Daily; Xi 2017 forum speech). So the "belt" is roads and rails over land; the "road" is sea lanes. (The names are counter-intuitive, a nice joke.)
- China says it has signed BRI cooperation documents with more than 150 countries and 30+ international organizations (CGTN, Sept 2026: China's own figure, attribute it).
- AidData "Banking on the Belt and Road" (Sept 29, 2021): 13,427 projects worth $843B in 165 countries, financed by 300+ Chinese state institutions, approved 2000-2017.
  - In an average BRI-era year, China's overseas development spending was about $85B vs about $37B for the US (AidData/W&M release, 2021). Roughly 2-to-1 (our math: 85/37 = 2.3).
  - Most BRI-era lending shifted from central governments to state-owned companies, state banks, special purpose vehicles and joint ventures (AidData brief, Malik & Parks, Nov 2021).
  - "Hidden debt": about $385B of debts not reported on government balance sheets; 42 countries have public debt exposure to China above 10% of GDP (AidData 2021). A narrower AidData estimate is $309B.
  - 35% of the BRI infrastructure project portfolio hit major implementation problems (scandals, labor violations, environmental hazards, protests) (AidData 2021).
  - Collateralized/guaranteed lending rose from roughly 30% of the portfolio in the early 2000s to nearly 60% in the BRI era (Parks, The Diplomat, 2021).

## How the loans work
- Main lenders: China Development Bank and Export-Import Bank of China (state policy banks) plus state-owned commercial banks. BU's CODF database: CDB and China Exim committed $472B in public and publicly guaranteed finance, 2008-2024, across 1,304 loans; since 2020 about $6.2B a year on average; $6.1B in 2024 (BU GDP Center, June 2025).
- Terms (AidData 2021 executive summary): average Chinese loan has a 4.2% interest rate, a grace period of less than two years, and a maturity of less than 10 years. A typical OECD-DAC lender (Germany, France, Japan) loan: about 1.1% interest and about 28 years (AidData comparison, as cited by secondary summaries and by Brad Parks to VOA in 2017: "about 1%", "28 years"). UNCERTAIN: other datasets (CGD; Lowy) show longer Chinese maturities (15-20 years) and grace periods (3-5 years). Say "on average, per AidData" and "simplified."
- Contracts: "How China Lends" (AidData, Kiel Institute, CGD, PIIE, March 2021): 100 Chinese loan contracts signed 2000-2020. Every contract since 2014 had a confidentiality clause; many had "no Paris Club" clauses (keeping Chinese debt out of group restructurings); broad rights to cancel or accelerate repayment.
- Rescue lending: AidData/World Bank/Kiel/Harvard "China as an International Lender of Last Resort" (March 2023): by end-2021, 128 rescue operations in 22 debtor countries worth $240B (about $170B via People's Bank of China swap lines; about $70B from state banks and firms). About 80% (more than $185B) issued 2016-2021. Rescue lending was $40.5B in 2021 (IMF: $68.6B that year). Lending to countries in debt distress rose from under 5% of China's overseas lending portfolio in 2010 to 60% in 2022 (TIME, Reuters coverage).

## Worked example (our math, simplified, ignores grace periods and fees)
- $1 billion loan, repaid in equal yearly payments.
  - Chinese average terms: 4.2% over 10 years. Payment = 1,000,000,000 x 0.042 / (1 - 1.042^-10) = about $124.5M a year. Total paid about $1.245B.
  - Typical Western aid-agency terms: 1.1% over 28 years. Payment = 1,000,000,000 x 0.011 / (1 - 1.011^-28) = about $41.7M a year. Total about $1.167B.
  - Yearly squeeze: 124.5 / 41.7 = about 3 times bigger each year, even though total interest differs less (about $245M vs $167M). Surprise: speed of repayment, not just rate, is what pinches a budget.
- Comparison to your life: like a 10-year car-style payoff vs a 28-year mortgage-style payoff on the same amount.

## Case studies
1. Sri Lanka, Hambantota port
   - Built with China Exim loans. In 2017, Sri Lanka leased a 70% stake in the port company to China Merchants Port Holdings for 99 years for $1.12B (The Diplomat, Jan 2020; SAIS-CARI briefing paper; deal agreed July 2017, handed over December 2017). (Some sources cite 85% of the holding company: use 70%.)
   - The lease did not cancel the port debt; Sri Lanka used the cash to help with its dollar shortfall (The Diplomat 2020; SAIS-CARI). Port assets were not collateral (SAIS-CARI).
   - Sri Lanka suspended external debt payments April 12, 2022, its first default since independence (missed bond coupons; formal default May 2022). China held roughly 10% of its external debt (World Bank estimate cited "up to 12%" of $63B; commonly cited 10-15% of public external debt). Bondholders were the largest group, about 36% (9DashLine/South Asian Voices). UNCERTAIN: share depends on measure.
2. Zambia
   - Missed a Eurobond coupon in November 2020, the first African sovereign default of the pandemic era (Oxford Law blog, Oct 2025; ODI; Reuters).
   - June 2023: $6.3B restructuring deal with official creditors (committee co-chaired by China and France) under the G20 Common Framework (Reuters; Credendo). China is Zambia's largest official creditor (~30% of external debt per Oxford blog estimate). Bondholder deal followed in 2024.
3. Kenya, Standard Gauge Railway (Mombasa-Nairobi)
   - Opened 2017 (passenger service May 31, 2017). China Exim financed about 90% of the cost; Exim loans about $3.2B (Ksh 324B) (Africa Confidential; total project cost cited $3.6-3.8B). Loan agreement signed May 11, 2014.
   - October 2025: Kenya converted three China Exim SGR loans (about $3.5B) from dollars to yuan; Treasury says it saves about $215M a year; rates from 6%+ (floating, dollar) to about 3% (yuan) (Bloomberg, Oct 7, 2025; bne IntelliNews; Business Daily). AidData argued savings came mostly from restructuring terms.
4. Pakistan, CPEC (mention briefly)
   - China-Pakistan Economic Corridor, announced 2015; plan widely reported at about $62B by 2018 (Heritage; Atlantic Council: ~$60B). Pakistan asked China in July 2024 to reschedule power-project debt as dues rose to Rs401B (Express Tribune). AidData rescue lending: Pakistan received $48.5B of China's rescue loans (TIME, 2023).

## The "debt-trap" debate (present both sides)
- Critics' points: Hambantota lease became the symbol; secrecy clauses; collateral; shorter maturities; "hidden debt" ($385B); about 35% of projects hit major problems (AidData). Pakistan and Kenya repayment strain.
- Researchers' counter-evidence:
  - Chatham House (Lee Jones and Shahar Hameiri, August 19, 2020), "Debunking the Myth of 'Debt-trap Diplomacy'": in Sri Lanka and Malaysia, the two most cited "victims," the controversial projects were driven largely by the recipient governments; Hambantota was a long-standing Rajapaksa family plan.
  - Deborah Brautigam and Meg Rithmire (The Atlantic, Feb 2021, "The Chinese 'Debt Trap' Is a Myth"); follow-up with Won Kidane found no Chinese asset seizures in sovereign lending. Rhodium Group (2019) found outright asset seizures are rare (Sri Lanka lease; a Tajikistan case).
  - BU GDP Center (Gallagher et al.): eight cited cases, no empirical evidence of lending aimed at seizing strategic assets.
- Wider context: IMF (2025 policy paper): about half of low-income countries are at high risk of, or already in, debt distress. IMF list as of September 30, 2025: 9 in distress, 24 at high risk out of 67 (our math: 33/67 = ~49%). Kevin Gallagher (Al Jazeera, 2025): remove China and many poor countries would still be in distress.
- Fair summary: research finds little evidence of a deliberate asset-grab plan; it does find expensive, secretive, fast-repaying loans that strained some borrowers. Both can be true.

In 2025, developing countries owe a record $35B in debt service to China; the 75 poorest countries $22B. In 2012 China was a net drain (more repayments than new loans) on 18 developing countries; by 2023, 60. Chinese foreign ministry rejected the framing.

## How it reaches an American's wallet
- Money flows into the US: AidData (Nov 2025) says the US was the largest recipient of Chinese state-sector lending, nearly $202B, 2000-2023, mostly loans to companies and projects (coverage mentions LNG projects, pipelines, data centers, airport terminals; UNCERTAIN on named firms, avoid naming).
- Commodities and batteries: Chinese lending and investment helped build mines, ports and railways that move raw materials. Example: Congo's Sicomines "minerals-for-infrastructure" deal (2008; revised in 2024 with up to $7B for infrastructure, Chinese partners 68%, Gecamines 32%; spending tied to copper above $8,000/t) (Reuters via Mining.com, Jan/Mar 2024). Congo is the world's top cobalt miner, roughly 70%+ of world output (USGS-based reporting; UNCERTAIN exact %, say "about seven in ten"). Cobalt goes into phone and EV batteries.
- Cheaper ports and rail can lower shipping costs for goods; debt crises can disrupt supply of commodities (general mechanism; no number).
- Western response: G7 "Partnership for Global Infrastructure and Investment" (PGII), launched June 2022, goal to mobilize $600B by 2027; US share $200B through grants, federal finance and private investment (White House fact sheets 2022, 2024). Flagship: Lobito Corridor rail to Angola's Atlantic port of Lobito, linking the copper belt (Congo, Zambia); by end-2025 about $4.5B allocated by the US and about EUR 2B by the EU (PISM 2026; UNCERTAIN).
- Neutral framing: both are competing ways of financing infrastructure; the video explains, does not judge.
