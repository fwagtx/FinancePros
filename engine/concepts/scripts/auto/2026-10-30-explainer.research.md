# 2026-10-30-explainer research brief: Credit limit increases - what asking for one does to your score
Researched October 1, 2026 (posts October 30, 2026). Evergreen; no news hook needed. Note: issuer/bureau/FICO/CFPB/NY Fed pages were blocked for direct fetch in this session, so wording below is confirmed via search-result excerpts of those official pages (no word-for-word quotes are used in the script).

## Core facts
1. Hard vs soft inquiries (CFPB, Ask CFPB "When will my lender run or obtain a copy of my credit report?" and related CFPB pages):
   - A hard inquiry is made by a lender when you apply for credit; hard inquiries can affect credit scores because most scoring models look at how recently and how often you applied for credit.
   - A soft inquiry is recorded when your file is reviewed for reasons other than an application, for example when a lender checks an existing account or you request your own report. Soft inquiries do not affect credit scores.
   - CFPB lists evaluating a request to increase your credit limit as one reason a lender may pull credit.
2. Issuers differ (Experian, "Does Getting Declined for a Credit Limit Increase Affect Your Credit?"): whether a limit-increase request affects scores depends on whether the issuer uses a hard or soft inquiry; issuers aren't always clear but some share it online; a single hard inquiry's impact is usually minimal / a temporary dip. Getting declined itself is not what affects the score; the inquiry type is.
3. Named issuer policy, from its own site (Capital One Help Center "Increasing your credit limit" and Capital One Learn & Grow "Do Credit Limit Increases Hurt Your Credit?"): Capital One uses soft inquiries to review credit limit increases, so credit scores aren't impacted, whether the cardholder requests the increase or Capital One offers it. (Policy as stated on capitalone.com, confirmed via search Oct 2026.)
   - American Express's own Credit Intel article ("Does Asking for a Credit Limit Increase Impact Credit Score?") says that when you request an increase, an issuer might run a hard inquiry, while some increases happen with only a soft inquiry. (General statement, not used as an Amex policy claim.)
4. Size/duration of a hard inquiry (myFICO): for most people one additional hard inquiry costs fewer than five FICO points; FICO Scores consider inquiries for 12 months; they can stay on the credit report up to 2 years. Inquiries are part of "new credit," 10% of a FICO Score. Amex Credit Intel echoes ~5 points at most on average.
5. FICO weights (myFICO): payment history 35%, amounts owed 30%, length of credit history 15%, new credit 10%, credit mix 10%. Utilization is part of amounts owed. Utilization = balance / credit limit. myFICO/Experian: lower utilization tends to be better; people with top scores tend to have low (single-digit) utilization; no hard cliff at 30%.
6. VantageScore 4.0 weights (vantagescore.com guide; Capital One and Chase education pages): payment history 41%, depth of credit 20%, credit utilization 20%, recent credit 11%, available credit 2%. (Percentages cited by secondary pages; VantageScore notes they are approximate, based on population studies.)

## Worked example (HYPOTHETICAL; assumes the balance stays the same and is the only card)
- Before: $1,500 balance / $5,000 limit = 0.30 = 30% utilization
- After a limit increase to $7,500, same $1,500 balance: $1,500 / $7,500 = 0.20 = 20%
- Limit rose $2,500 (50%); utilization fell 10 percentage points.

## The surprising fact
Asking for more credit doesn't necessarily touch your score at all: at Capital One the review is a soft inquiry. And the bigger score lever is usually not the inquiry (<5 pts, 10% category) but utilization (30% of FICO), which a higher limit lowers if the balance stays put.
