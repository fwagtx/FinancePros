## What it is
- Purchasing-power parity (PPP): the idea that, in the long run, exchange rates should move toward the rate that makes an identical basket of goods cost the same everywhere. The Big Mac index uses one "basket": a Big Mac.
- Introduced by The Economist in September 1986 (by Pam Woodall) as a lighthearted, semi-humorous guide to whether currencies are at their "correct" level. Gave rise to the word "burgernomics". (Wikipedia; Montclair State Univ. page reproducing The Economist's description; multiple glossaries.) The Economist says it was never meant to be a precise gauge.
- GDP-adjusted version introduced in 2011: fits a "line of best fit" between Big Mac prices and GDP per person, then compares each country's actual price with the price predicted for its income level. Rationale: burgers should be cheaper in poorer countries because labor is cheaper. (Economist via kottke 2011 / UConn O'Brien & Ruiz de Vargas paper.)
- July 2022 methodology changes (Economist GitHub README): US price is now a McDonald's-provided figure (previously the average of four US cities); GDP-adjusted method changed. Exchange rates from Refinitiv Datastream (July 2022 on); GDP per person from the IMF World Economic Outlook.
- July 2026 edition covers 54 entries (53 plus the US; euro area counted as one).

## Worked examples (exact math)
Japan (July 2026):
- Implied PPP rate = local price / US price = 500 / 6.22 = **80.39 yen per dollar**.
- Actual rate = 162.135 yen per dollar.
- Valuation = 80.39 / 162.135 - 1 = **-50.4%** (yen undervalued by about half).
- Same thing in dollars: 500 / 162.135 = $3.08; $3.08 / $6.22 - 1 = -50.4%.

Switzerland (July 2026):
- Implied rate = 7.30 / 6.22 = **1.17 francs per dollar**; actual 0.80735.
- 1.1736 / 0.80735 - 1 = **+45.4%** (franc overvalued).
- Dollar price 7.30 / 0.80735 = $9.04.

Surprising fact: the same burger costs $9.04 in Switzerland and $3.08 in Japan (July 2026 edition) -- nearly three times as much (9.04 / 3.08 = 2.94). Japan's Big Mac costs ¥500; in July 2016 it cost ¥370.

## Limitations
- A Big Mac is not fully tradable: price includes local rent, wages, electricity, local taxes (VAT/sales tax), and competition/market positioning -- none of which can be shipped across borders.
- Richer countries have higher wages, so non-traded services cost more (why the GDP-adjusted index exists).
- PPP is a long-run idea; currencies can stay "misvalued" for years (yen undervalued in every July edition since 2013). Not a forecasting tool; no predictions.
- Exchange rates in the index are a snapshot from around the survey date.

## How it reaches an American's wallet (education, no advice)
- Travel: when the dollar buys a lot of yen (162 per $1 at the July 2026 survey), US travelers' dollars stretch further in Japan; Switzerland feels expensive.
- Imports: a stronger dollar tends to make imported goods cheaper for Americans and US exports pricier abroad; a weaker dollar does the opposite. (General PPP/exchange-rate mechanics.)
- The US price rose from $2.24 (April 2000) to $6.22 (July 2026) -- a reminder that inflation in dollars is separate from currency value.
