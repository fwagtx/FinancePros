## Key facts (with dates and sources)
- 50/30/20: popularized by Elizabeth Warren (then a Harvard Law professor, now a US senator) and her daughter Amelia Warren Tyagi in "All Your Worth: The Ultimate Lifetime Money Plan" (Free Press, March 2005; trade paperback 2006). Split of AFTER-TAX income: 50% needs (must-haves), 30% wants, 20% savings and debt repayment. Sources: Acorns, Bankrate, Simon & Schuster listing (Mar 8, 2005), library catalogs. Note: Quicken/Acorns say it's debated whether Warren invented it, but she popularized it -> say "popularized".
  - Caveat (Bankrate): may not work well for very low or very high incomes; where housing is costly, needs can exceed 50%.
- Zero-based budgeting: income minus every assigned dollar = $0; savings and debt payoff are "jobs" too. Origin in business: Peter Pyhrr, a manager at Texas Instruments, developed it in the late 1960s and wrote an influential Harvard Business Review article, Nov-Dec 1970. Jimmy Carter later used it for Georgia's state budget (dates vary 1971/1973 - not used). Sources: Wikipedia (Peter Pyhrr), D Magazine Oct 1976, WallStreetMojo.
  - Trade-off: most control, most time (plan every month).
- Pay yourself first: move savings out first on payday, spend the rest. Phrase/idea popularized by George S. Clason's "The Richest Man in Babylon" (pamphlets from 1926, later collected as the book); its principle is keeping at least one-tenth (10%) of what you earn. Sources: Wikipedia (The Richest Man in Babylon; George Samuel Clason), Experian, The Citizen. (Experian says book compiled 1930; 1926 is the pamphlet/first-edition date per Wikipedia -> say "nineteen twenty-six" only with "pamphlets/book" care; script says "1926 classic" is acceptable per Wikipedia first-edition 1926.)
  - Trade-off: least tracking; but overspending in the remainder isn't tracked.

## Worked example (OUR OWN MATH, hypothetical $4,000/month take-home)
- 50/30/20: 4,000 x 0.50 = $2,000 needs; x 0.30 = $1,200 wants; x 0.20 = $800 savings/debt. Check 2,000+1,200+800 = 4,000.
- Zero-based (example jobs): bills/rent $2,000 + food & gas $600 + fun $500 + savings $700 + extra debt $200 = $4,000; unassigned = $0.
- Pay yourself first (10%, echoing Clason's one-tenth): 4,000 x 0.10 = $400 to savings on payday; 4,000 - 400 = $3,600 left to spend with no categories.

## Surprising fact
- Zero-based budgeting began as a corporate tool (Texas Instruments, HBR 1970), and "pay yourself first" traces to a 1926 parable book set in ancient Babylon.
