# Research brief: How the rich pay less tax, legally (2026-10-05-lf-explained)

Posts Monday, October 5, 2026. Evergreen: every number carries its date or period. Politically neutral: describe the rules and the math, no verdict on fairness.

## Timely angle
- Forbes 400 (published September 15, 2026): the 400 richest Americans are worth a combined $8 trillion, up from $6.6 trillion in 2025. Cutoff to make the list: a record $4.4 billion ($600 million more than 2025). A record 590 US billionaires did not make the list. (Forbes, Sept 15, 2026; Boston.com Sept 16, 2026; AP/Lee syndication)
- Most of that wealth is stock and other assets that have risen in value. Under US law, a rise in value is not taxed as income until the asset is sold ("realization").

## The surprising fact (two studies, with the definitional caveat)
- White House CEA + OMB report (Sept 23, 2021, Leiserson and Yagan): the 400 wealthiest families paid an average federal individual income tax rate of 8.2% from 2010 to 2018, when their income is measured to INCLUDE unrealized gains on their wealth. (bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov, Sept 23, 2021; CNBC, UPI Sept 23, 2021)
- ProPublica "Secret IRS Files" (June 8, 2021): 25 richest Americans' wealth rose $401 billion from 2014 to 2018; they paid federal income taxes equal to 3.4% of that wealth growth, which ProPublica called a "true tax rate". (ProPublica; CBS News; NPR, June 8, 2021)
- Critique (Tax Foundation, Erica York): the 8.2% figure "creates a new definition of income" applied only to the top 400, and leaves out corporate income taxes paid by the companies they own, which would raise the figure significantly. PolitiFact/FactCheck.org also noted it is not the rate under the law's normal definition of income.
- Neutral framing: the answer depends on whether "income" includes gains you have not sold.

## Key rules (2026 tax year)
- Ordinary income brackets, single, 2026 (IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32, Oct 2025; KPMG summary): 10% to $12,400; 12% to $50,400; 22% to $105,700; 24% to $201,775; 32% to $256,225; 35% to $640,600; 37% above $640,600. Standard deduction single: $16,100.
- 37% top rate made permanent by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025 (Tax Foundation FAQ; law-firm summaries).
- Long-term capital gains (held over 1 year), 2026, single: 0% up to $49,450 of taxable income; 15% up to $545,500; 20% above. Married filing jointly: 0% to $98,900, 20% above $613,700. (Rev. Proc. 2025-32; Kiplinger)
- Net investment income tax: 3.8% on investment income above MAGI $200,000 single / $250,000 joint; thresholds NOT indexed for inflation since enacted (ACA 2010, effective 2013). (IRS; CRS IF11820)
- Top federal rate on long-term gains: 20% + 3.8% = 23.8%.
- Payroll taxes 2026: Social Security 6.2% (employee) on wages up to $184,500 (SSA, Oct 2025); max employee SS tax $11,439. Medicare 1.45% on all wages, plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages above $200,000 single. Investment gains pay no payroll tax.
- Top marginal federal rate on wages above $200,000 (employee side, 2026): 37% + 1.45% + 0.9% = 39.35% vs 23.8% on long-term gains.
- Step-up in basis (IRC section 1014): heirs' cost basis resets to market value at death; the gain during the owner's life is never subject to income tax.
- JCT (JCX-45-25, Estimates of Federal Tax Expenditures FY2025-2029): step-up of basis at death = about $72.5 billion of forgone revenue in FY2026; reduced rates on capital gains and dividends about $252 billion in FY2026 (CRFB summary). PGPF also cites $72.5B for 2026.
- Estate and gift tax, 2026: $15 million lifetime exemption per person ($30 million for a married couple with portability), permanent under OBBBA, indexed for inflation from 2027. Annual gift exclusion: $19,000 per recipient in 2026. (IRS via Morgan Lewis, Oct 2025)
- Retirement accounts 2026: 401(k) employee limit $24,500; IRA $7,500 (IRS news release, Nov 13, 2025).
- QSBS (section 1202): for qualified small business stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the gain exclusion cap is the greater of $15 million or 10x basis (OBBBA; Katten, Ropes & Gray).
- Municipal bond interest: generally exempt from federal income tax (IRC section 103).
- Borrowing: loan proceeds are not income, so borrowing against a stock portfolio is not a taxable event (general rule; NYU Law Review 2024 "Taxing 'Borrow' in 'Buy/Borrow/Die'").

## History (timeline)
- 1913: 16th Amendment; modern income tax.
- 1921: Revenue Act of 1921 creates a lower 12.5% rate for capital gains on assets held 2+ years (from 1922) and codifies stepped-up basis at death. (CRS; ACTEC; Columbia Blue Sky blog)
- 2013: 3.8% net investment income tax begins; top gains rate 20% -> 23.8% with NIIT.
- June 2024: Moore v. United States. Supreme Court upheld the tax at issue narrowly and did not decide whether income must be "realized" to be taxed. (Cornell LII; DLA Piper)
- July 4, 2025: OBBBA; 37% top rate permanent; estate exemption $15M.

## Worked example 1 (hypothetical, 2026, single filer, standard deduction only, no other income, federal only)
Same $100,000, two sources.
- Worker, $100,000 wages: taxable = 100,000 - 16,100 = 83,900. Tax = 10% x 12,400 (1,240) + 12% x 38,000 (4,560) + 22% x 33,500 (7,370) = $13,170. Payroll: 6.2% x 100,000 = 6,200; 1.45% x 100,000 = 1,450. Total $20,820 (20.8%).
- Investor, $100,000 long-term gains: taxable 83,900. 0% on first 49,450; 15% x 34,450 = $5,167.50. NIIT: none (under $200,000). Total $5,167.50 (about 5.2%).
Same $1,000,000:
- Wages: income tax $320,000.25 on taxable 983,900; Social Security $11,439; Medicare 1.45% x 1,000,000 = 14,500 + 0.9% x 800,000 = 7,200 -> 21,700. Total $353,139.25 (35.3%).
- Long-term gains: 0% on 49,450; 15% x 496,050 = 74,407.50; 20% x 438,400 = 87,680; gains tax $162,087.50; NIIT 3.8% x 800,000 = 30,400. Total $192,487.50 (19.2%).

## Worked example 2: buy, borrow, die (hypothetical)
- Stock bought for $1 million, now worth $10 million. Gain $9 million.
- Sell it all while alive: 23.8% x $9,000,000 = $2,142,000 federal tax (top rates assumed).
- Borrow against it instead: loan is not income, $0 income tax on the loan. Interest is a real cost (example assumes a rate, e.g. 6% -> $60,000 a year per $1 million borrowed).
- Hold until death: heirs' basis steps up to $10 million; the $9 million gain is never subject to income tax. Estate under $15 million exemption (2026) owes no federal estate tax.
