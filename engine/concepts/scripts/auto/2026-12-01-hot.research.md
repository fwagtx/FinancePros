# 2026-12-01-hot research brief: Myth: "full coverage" car insurance covers everything

Researched October 2, 2026. Direct fetch of bankrate.com, iii.org and insurance-research.org was blocked by the network proxy; every figure below is confirmed from search results quoting the original source, cross-checked where possible.

No timely news hook needed: evergreen money myth.

## Core facts
1. "Full coverage" is not an official policy type. There is "no exact or standard definition of 'full coverage' automobile insurance in the insurance industry, or in state law" (West Virginia Offices of the Insurance Commissioner, Bulletin 21-02, 2021). It usually means liability (plus other state-required coverages) + collision + comprehensive (Allstate, Progressive, GEICO consumer guides; Insurance Information Institute usage).
2. Average cost of full coverage: $2,697 per year (Bankrate 2026 national average, Quadrant Information Services data; about $225/month). Bankrate's 2025 True Cost of Auto Insurance report: $2,638 in 2025, up 12% from $2,349 in 2024; $2,013 in 2023.
3. Liability has limits. The most common state minimum is 25/50/25 (2026): $25,000 bodily injury per person, $50,000 bodily injury per accident, $25,000 property damage. Required in states including Georgia, Ohio, Tennessee, Missouri, Oklahoma (CNBC Select / CarInsurance.com 2026 state-minimum guides). Costs above the limits are not paid by the policy.
4. What "full coverage" typically does NOT include:
   - The loan gap: collision/comprehensive pay actual cash value (ACV) minus the deductible; gap insurance (separate add-on) covers the difference to the loan balance (Progressive, State Farm, Forbes Advisor). Gap usually does not cover the deductible.
   - Rental reimbursement and towing/roadside: optional add-ons (Progressive, Allstate, CNBC Select).
   - Wear and tear / mechanical breakdown: not covered by standard auto insurance (CNBC Select "13 surprising things car insurance doesn't cover"; Progressive).
   - Rideshare driving: personal policies exclude livery/for-hire use; an endorsement is needed (NAIC via search; insurer guides).
   - Medical and damage costs above liability limits (see 3).
   - Aftermarket/custom parts: many policies cap custom equipment at about $1,000 unless extra coverage is added (insurer guides; weakly sourced, not used in script).
5. Uninsured drivers: 15.4% of motorists, about 1 in 7, were uninsured in 2023 (Insurance Research Council, Feb/March 2025 study "Uninsured and Underinsured Motorists: 2017-2023"; reported by Triple-I blog, Carrier Management, BusinessWire). 33.4% (1 in 3) were uninsured or underinsured; 18.0% underinsured. State range 5.7% (Maine) to 28.2% (Mississippi).
6. UM/UIM (uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage) is a separate coverage, not one of collision/comprehensive/liability. Required in about 20+ jurisdictions (e.g., New York, Illinois, Virginia), optional elsewhere (insurer/legislative summaries; count varies by source, so script avoids the exact count).

## Worked example (hypothetical, stated assumptions)
Car is totaled. Actual cash value $20,000. Loan balance $25,000. Collision deductible $500. No gap insurance.
- Insurer pays ACV - deductible = $20,000 - $500 = $19,500 (paid toward the loan).
- Still owed = $25,000 - $19,500 = $5,500 (the $5,000 gap + the $500 deductible).

## Chartable series (checked, NOT used: narrow range from a 0 baseline)
- Bankrate average full-coverage premium: 2023 $2,013; 2024 $2,349; 2025 $2,638; 2026 $2,697 (methodology changes year to year; range too narrow for a 0-based chart).
- IRC uninsured motorist rate: 2017 12.4%, 2018 12.1%, 2019 11.6%, 2020 14.3%, 2021 14.6%, 2022 15.2%, 2023 15.4% (via search summary; narrow range). Script uses stat/compare/list scenes instead.

## The surprise
"Full coverage" can pay out on a totaled car and still leave the driver owing thousands ($5,500 in the example), and it isn't a defined product at all.
