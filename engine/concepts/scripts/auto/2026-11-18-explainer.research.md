# Research brief: Price elasticity (why some prices can rise without people buying less)
Slot: 2026-11-18-explainer (posts Nov 18, 2026). Researched Oct 2026. Evergreen, no news hook needed.

## Definition
- Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity bought / % change in price.
- Absolute value below 1 = "inelastic" (quantity moves less than price); above 1 = "elastic".
- When demand is inelastic, a price increase raises total spending (price x quantity), i.e., sellers' revenue rises.

## Key real example: US gasoline, 2021 vs 2022 (EIA)
- US regular gasoline retail price, annual average: 2021 $3.01/gal; 2022 $3.95/gal (EIA, Today in Energy / This Week in Petroleum Jan 2023).
  - Change: (3.95 - 3.01) / 3.01 = +31.2% -> "about 31%".
- US finished motor gasoline consumption: 2021 about 8.82 million b/d (370.3 million gal/day); 2022 about 8.78 million b/d (EIA FAQ "How much gasoline does the US consume?"; EIA product-supplied table shows ~8.81). Either way: a decline of under 1% (about 0.1%-0.5%).
  - Caveat: 2021-2022 also includes post-pandemic driving recovery, so this is an illustration, not a clean elasticity estimate.

## Academic estimates (gasoline)
- Hughes, Knittel & Sperling (NBER WP 12530, 2006; Energy Journal 2008): short-run price elasticity of US gasoline demand -0.034 to -0.077 in 2001-2006, vs -0.21 to -0.34 in 1975-1980. Surprise fact: drivers became LESS price-sensitive over time.
- EIA literature review (STEO workshop, Sept 2017): short-run estimates are small, roughly -0.02 to -0.1 range in recent studies; Dahl & Sterner (1991) survey average -0.26.

## Cigarettes
- A 10% increase in cigarette price reduces adult consumption about 3% to 5% (National Cancer Institute Tobacco Control Monograph 14; CDC Grand Rounds, MMWR 2010: ~4% in US). Implies elasticity about -0.3 to -0.5: inelastic. Youth are more price-sensitive (Monograph 14).

## Worked example (HYPOTHETICAL, exact math)
- Gas goes from $4.00 to $4.40 a gallon: +10% ((4.40-4.00)/4.00 = 0.10).
- A driver buys 10 gallons a week, then 9.9 gallons: -1% ((9.9-10)/10 = -0.01).
- Elasticity = -1% / 10% = -0.1 -> inelastic (absolute value below 1).
- Weekly spending: before 10 x $4.00 = $40.00; after 9.9 x $4.40 = $43.56 (+8.9%). Seller revenue rises.

## Why demand is inelastic (definitional, textbook)
- Few quick substitutes (commute needs gas), necessity / habit, short time window. Over longer periods people adjust (more efficient cars), so long-run elasticity is larger.

## Surprising fact
- Gas prices jumped about 31% in 2022 and US gasoline use fell less than 1%.
