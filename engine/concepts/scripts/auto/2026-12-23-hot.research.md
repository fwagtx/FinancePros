# Research brief: 2026-12-23-hot (Hot Take)
Topic: What happens if you die with money in a 401(k) and no beneficiary?
Researched Saturday, October 24, 2026. Posts Wednesday, December 23, 2026, 6 PM Central. Evergreen: no news peg used.
Education only, not legal advice. Format: Money Myth ("my will decides who gets my 401(k)") -> fact.

## Core facts (with sources)
1. **The plan document / beneficiary form governs, not the will.** ERISA plans must be administered "in accordance with the documents and instruments governing the plan" (ERISA sec. 404(a)(1)(D), quoted in Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141 (2001)). The Supreme Court's "plan documents rule" (Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont, 555 U.S. 285 (2009)): the administrator looks to the plan documents and the designation on file. A will only matters if the money ends up in the estate. (Sources: Cornell LII case text/summaries; Crowell & Moring and McGuireWoods client alerts on Kennedy, 2009.)
2. **Married participant: spouse is generally the default beneficiary.** Under ERISA / IRC sec. 417(a)(2), a 401(k) participant's spouse is generally entitled to the death benefit unless the spouse consents in writing to a different beneficiary; the consent must acknowledge its effect and be witnessed by a notary public or a plan representative. Courts enforce this strictly (8th Cir. 2013: postnup did not waive spousal 401(k) rights; a prenup does not count because the signer was not yet a spouse). (Sources: Nolo; Tax Notes on IRC 417(a)(2)(A)(iii); Pension Rights Center comment letters 2021/2023; FindLaw 8th Cir. summary.)
3. **No beneficiary on file: the plan's default order applies.** Plans set their own order; a common one is spouse, then children, then the participant's estate. If it goes to the estate it generally passes through probate, and if there is no will, state intestacy law decides the heirs. Plans vary: the summary plan description controls. (Sources: SmartAsset, Western & Southern, Ed Slott/IRAhelp Q&A — secondary; note "plans vary".)
4. **Estate as beneficiary = shorter payout clock.** An estate is not an individual "designated beneficiary." If the participant died BEFORE the required beginning date (RBD) for RMDs, the 5-year rule applies: the account must be emptied by the end of the 5th year after the year of death (no annual RMDs needed in years 1-4). If death is ON/AFTER the RBD, payouts generally continue over the decedent's remaining single life expectancy ("ghost" rule) — so the 5-year rule is only for pre-RBD deaths. Most individual non-spouse designated beneficiaries (since the SECURE Act, deaths after 2019) get the 10-year rule: empty by end of 10th year after death (with annual RMDs in some cases when death was after RBD). Plans can require faster payout (some require a lump sum). Spouses have extra options (e.g., rollover / treat as own). (Sources: IRS "Retirement topics - Beneficiary" page (definitions of 5-year and 10-year rules); ThinkAdvisor Tax Facts, Sept 2026, "RMDs when a client's estate is their IRA beneficiary"; Wolters Kluwer June 2021; Kitces / WealthManagement on 2024 final RMD regs.)
5. **The surprise: the form can beat a divorce.**
   - **Egelhoff v. Egelhoff, 532 U.S. 141, decided March 21, 2001 (7-2, Thomas, J.).** David Egelhoff named wife Donna Rae on his Boeing pension plan and life insurance (both ERISA). They divorced April 1994; he died about two months later. His children sued under a Washington law that automatically revoked a spouse's designation on divorce. Supreme Court: ERISA preempts that state law as applied to ERISA plans; the ex-wife on the form prevailed. (Cornell LII; Wikipedia case box; Wiggin and Dana alert.)
   - **Kennedy v. Plan Administrator for DuPont Savings and Investment Plan, 555 U.S. 285, decided January 26, 2009 (9-0).** William Kennedy's ex-wife Liv waived her interest in his DuPont savings plan in their divorce decree, but he never changed the beneficiary form. The plan paid Liv; the Court upheld it: the plan documents and the designation on file control. (Crowell & Moring; McGuireWoods 2009 alert; The Daily Record 2009.)
   - ERISA itself was signed into law September 2, 1974.
6. **Data point (survey, label as survey):** Caring.com 2025 Wills and Estate Planning Study (with YouGov, 2,500+ US adults): 24% of respondents said they have a will, down from 33% in 2022. Relevance: if a 401(k) defaults to the estate and there is no will, state law picks the heirs.
   Chartable series (Caring.com survey, % of respondents with a will): 2022: 33%, 2023: 34%, 2024: 32%, 2025: 24%. (2023/2024 figures from secondary coverage of Caring.com; 2025 and 2022 from Caring.com page, updated Feb 11, 2026.) No 2026 edition found as of Oct 24, 2026.
   - WEAK / NOT USED: share of 401(k) participants with no beneficiary on file. Only a single recordkeeper's estimate ("about a third" of its participants, PLANSPONSOR) and a PSCA sponsor poll (2023: only a quarter of sponsors said most participants had a valid form). Not national; not used on screen.

## Worked example (hypothetical, logic only — no tax math used)
A single 45-year-old dies with a 401(k), no beneficiary on file, before RMDs began. Plan default -> estate. Estate as non-designated beneficiary -> 5-year rule: death in 2026 -> must be fully paid out by Dec 31, 2031 (2026 + 5). If instead a sibling had been named, the 10-year rule: by Dec 31, 2036 (2026 + 10). Plan terms may require an earlier lump sum.

## The surprising fact
A divorce decree and a state divorce-revocation law did not remove an ex-spouse from an ERISA plan: the Supreme Court (2001, 2009) said the form on file controls.
