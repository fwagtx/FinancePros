## History
- 1970s: oil shocks (1973 embargo, 1979). Paul Volcker's Fed (Oct 6, 1979 anti-inflation measures). Fed funds reached a record 20% in late 1980 (Federal Reserve History essay). Inflation fell to about 6% by early 1982; unemployment peaked at 10.8% in late 1982 (Fed History). 12-month CPI peaked at 14.8% in March 1980 (BLS CPI-U; widely cited; Fed History essay uses a different measure, 11.6% -- not used on screen).
- Mortgage peak: Freddie Mac 30-year 18.63% in October 1981 (Freddie Mac, cited widely).
- 2021-22: record-low mortgage 2.65% (Freddie Mac, week of Jan 7, 2021). CPI 12-month +9.1% in June 2022, largest since November 1981 (BLS TED). Core June 2022: 5.9% (BLS release, July 13, 2022). Energy +41.6%, gasoline +59.9% y/y (June 2022). Fed hiked 11 times from near zero (March 2022) to 5.25%-5.50% (July 26, 2023), highest in over 22 years. 2022: S&P 500 -19.4% (AP/FactSet; -18.1% with dividends); Bloomberg US Aggregate bond index down more than 12%, worst year in its history (Axios, Dec 31, 2022). Nasdaq -33.1%.
- Key contrast now: headline 3.7% but core 2.6% (gas-driven); June 2022 core was 5.9%. Fed funds 3.75-4.00% vs CPI 3.7% -> "real" rate near zero.

## Worked examples (hypothetical, my math)
1. Paycheck: $50,000 salary, 3% raise -> $51,500. Keeping pace with 3.7% prices -> $51,850. Gap $350.
2. Savings: $10,000 at 0.38% (FDIC national average savings rate, July 2026 via FRED SNDR; Sept 21 release reportedly 0.37%) earns $38 in a year. Prices +3.7% -> same stuff costs $370 more. At an example 4% rate: $400 interest.
3. Rule of 72: 72 / 3.7 = 19.5 years for prices to double if 3.7% held (math illustration, not forecast).
4. Mortgage: $300,000, 30-yr, P&I: 5.98% -> $1,795/mo; 7.40% -> $2,077/mo; +$282.
5. Credit card: $5,000 at 22.15% (Fed G.19, Q2 2026, accounts assessed interest) -> ~$1,107.50 a year simple. 0.25 pt hike on $5,000 = $12.50/yr.
6. COLA: $2,000 benefit x 3.6% = $72/month (CNBC/ABC example).
7. Fixed-rate debt: a fixed $2,077 payment stays fixed while prices and (eventually) pay rise.

## What it means (descriptive)
- Paychecks: average pay up 3.0%, prices 3.7% -> real -0.7%.
- Savings: national average savings rate far below inflation; higher-rate accounts/Treasury bills closer to it.
- Debt: variable-rate (cards, HELOCs) costs rise with Fed hikes; fixed-rate borrowers locked in.
- 401(k): 2022 showed stocks and bonds can fall together when inflation and rates rise. History, not a prediction.
- Retirees: COLA 3.6% follows CPI-W.
- Winners/losers: savers in higher-yield accounts, banks' interest income, fixed-rate homeowners vs. renters/buyers, variable-rate borrowers, wage earners whose pay lags.
