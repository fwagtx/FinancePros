## Hook (true, specific)
- "Your dollar was copied from a Spanish silver coin." The Coinage Act of 1792 defined the US dollar as "of the value of a Spanish milled dollar as the same is now current", containing 371 4/16 grains of pure silver (416 grains standard silver). Source: Coinage Act of April 2, 1792, sec. 9 (text via Wikipedia "Coinage Act of 1792"; Econlib, Laughlin, "The Silver Period 1792-1834"). Hamilton set the weight from a Treasury assay of worn Spanish dollars.

## Key facts
### Potosí
- Silver found at Cerro Rico, Potosí (today's Bolivia) in 1545; tradition credits Diego Huallpa, an Indigenous Andean man (Real Academia de la Historia biography; Library of Congress blog 2022).
- Population: around 1600 about 160,000 people, largest city in the Americas and among the largest in the world (Americas Society/Council of the Americas exhibition "Potosí: Colonial Treasures"; other estimates up to 200,000). Say "historians estimate".
- Peak year 1592: "some 7.1 million ounces of silver were declared at the treasury office", refined in 80 water-driven mills (Encyclopedia of Latin American History and Culture, "Potosí" entry, via Encyclopedia.com). Another figure for 1592: 887,448 marks. Output leveled off around 1600 and declined slowly for over a century as rich surface ores ran out.
- Mercury amalgamation (introduced from Mexico on Viceroy Toledo's orders, 1570s); Peru's Huancavelica mercury mines supplied it. From 1575 to 1590 amalgamation multiplied Potosí's output about sixfold (Wikipedia global silver trade article summary; treat as approximate).
- Potosí mint opened 1572 (National Mint of Bolivia). Cervantes used the phrase "vale un Potosí" ("worth a Potosí", i.e., a fortune).
### Forced labor (handle respectfully)
- Mita: forced labor draft organized by Viceroy Francisco de Toledo in 1573; required about 13,500 Indigenous men a year from 16 provinces to work in Potosí (Scielo Chile article "La mita de Potosí"; Universidad del Pacífico glossary).
- Killers: accidents, silicosis, mercury poisoning (Kris Lane, "Potosí: The Silver City That Changed the World", 2019, per reviews). Encyclopedia of Latin American History and Culture: disease was "by far the larger destroyer of Indians".
- Enslaved Africans (largely from Angola and Congo) did much of the work in the Potosí mint, including smelting and turning the mills alongside mules (Harvard DASH thesis "Minting Slavery in the Colonial Andes"; Lane).
- Death toll: debated. Potosí chronicler Bartolomé Arzáns de Orsúa y Vela (early 1700s) claimed the mines had consumed eight million lives; many historians consider this far too high and estimate hundreds of thousands, noting 8 million may reflect the whole viceroyalty. Present as contested.
### The coin
- Mexico City mint: established 1535, first mint in the Americas; by the 1600s the New World's largest coin producer (Mexican Mint history).
- The 8-real coin = "piece of eight" = "Spanish dollar". Historians Alejandra Irigoin and Bridget Millmore (University of London Press, "New World Objects of Knowledge") call it the first global currency.
- Ward Barrett ("World Bullion Flows, 1450-1800", Cambridge 1990): from 1493 to 1800, about 85% of the world's silver came from the Americas.
- Price revolution: Earl J. Hamilton (1934) linked American treasure to inflation in Spain; later historians debate the size of that role.
### China and the Pacific
- Ming "single whip" reform, implemented nationwide by Zhang Juzheng in 1581: land and labor taxes combined into one payment assessed in silver (EBSCO Research Starters; Encyclopedia.com).
- Flynn & Giráldez ("China and the Spanish Empire", 1996; "Born with a Silver Spoon", Journal of World History 1995): from 1592 to the early 1600s gold:silver in Canton ran 1:5.5 to 1:7 vs 1:12.5 to 1:14 in Spain; silver was worth about twice as much in China. They date the start of continuous world trade to the founding of Manila in 1571.
- Manila galleons: 1565 to 1815, Manila-Acapulco, after Andrés de Urdaneta found the return route in 1565; the last galleon sailed in 1815. Silver went west, silks and porcelain east.
- Japan: about a third of the world's silver in the 16th century (Japan's Iwami Ginzan, per Japan's official "Japan Up Close"/Shimane material). Use "by some estimates".
- Yuan and yen were minted to Spanish/Mexican dollar specs; both names mean "round".

## Worked example (Flynn & Giráldez's own illustration)
- 1 oz gold buys about 11 oz silver in Amsterdam -> ship 11 oz silver to China, where gold:silver ~1:5.5 -> about 2 oz gold (11 / 5.5 = 2) -> back in Europe, 2 oz gold x 11 = 22 oz silver. Silver doubled, before shipping costs and risk.
- Second: 1792 dollar silver content: 371.25 grains / 480 grains per troy ounce = 0.773 troy oz (about 24.06 grams; 371.25 x 0.0648 g).
- "Two bits": a real ("bit") = 1/8 dollar = 12.5 cents; 2 x 12.5 = 25 cents = a quarter.

## American wallet
- Dollar sign: Bureau of Engraving and Printing FAQ: most widely accepted explanation is that $ evolved from the Mexican or Spanish "Ps" for pesos/piastres/pieces of eight; S written over P.
- Coinage Act 1792 (above).
- Act of April 10, 1806 allowed foreign coins like the Spanish dollar as legal tender; Coinage Act of Feb 21, 1857 ended legal tender status of foreign coins (signed by President Franklin Pierce).
- "Two bits" = quarter (CoinWeek; Encyclopedia.com "Pieces of Eight").
- US stock prices quoted in fractions (eighths, sixteenths) until decimalization; NYSE fully decimal Jan 29, 2001; all markets by April 2001 (GAO-05-535). The eighths habit is commonly traced to the Spanish dollar.

## Surprising fact
- In the 1590s, silver was worth roughly twice as much in China as in Spain (Flynn & Giráldez), so silver from the Andes ended up in China's tax system.
