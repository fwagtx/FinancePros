# 2027-01-09-explainer research: Keeping up with the Joneses

Evergreen; posts Sat Jan 9, 2027. No timely news hook used (all facts dated). Avoided recent explainers (25x rule, acorns crash,
rent-to-own, credit score isn't wealth).

## Core facts (verified)

1. Agarwal, Mikhed & Scholnick, "Does the Relative Income of Peers Cause Financial Distress? Evidence from Lottery Winners and
   Neighboring Bankruptcies," Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia Working Paper 18-16, May 2018 (published as "Peers' Income and
   Financial Distress," Review of Financial Studies 33(1), 2020, pp. 433-472).
   - Primary quote (WP 18-16, read via Firecrawl): "a lottery win equal to the median annual income in our sample (C$29,229)
     increases bankruptcies of neighbors in the three years after the win by 0.03, which is a 6.59% increase relative to the
     average bankruptcy rate of 0.455 in all postal codes in this event window."
   - Bloomberg (May 29, 2018) reports the same as about 6.6%.
   - Abstract: the dollar size of one neighbor's lottery win increases subsequent borrowing AND bankruptcies among other neighbors.
   - Neighbors = same six-digit Canadian postal code, median about 13 households (WEF 2016 summary; Bloomberg 2018).
   - Visible vs hidden: the size of the neighbor's win is related to the dollar value of MORE VISIBLE assets (house, car) of
     neighboring bankruptcy filers, not less visible ones (furniture) (working paper; WEF/Bloomberg coverage). Evidence of
     conspicuous consumption.
   - Effects stronger in lower-income areas and places with more inequality (coverage).
   - Caveat: bankruptcy is rare to begin with; the 6.6% is a RELATIVE increase, not percentage points.
   - Note: an earlier 2016 version was reported as "each C$1,000 of prize raises neighbors' bankruptcy odds 2.4%" (WEF 2016).
     NOT used: it comes from an earlier draft and secondary coverage.
   - Sample details (7,337 prizes, one province, 2004-2014, prizes C$1,000-C$150,000) appear in secondary summaries only; not used.

2. Kuhn, Kooreman, Soetevent & Kapteyn, "The Effects of Lottery Prizes on Winners and Their Neighbors: Evidence from the Dutch
   Postcode Lottery," American Economic Review 101(5), 2011, pp. 2226-2247 (IZA DP 4950, 2010).
   - Each week the lottery picks a postal code and gives cash plus a new BMW to participants there.
   - Abstract: nonparticipants who live next door to winners "have significantly higher levels of car consumption than other
     nonparticipants." Social effects also found for exterior home renovations.
   - Winners' own extra spending was largely on cars and other durables; most BMW winners sold the BMW.
   - NO bankruptcy finding in this study (the brief prompt's "higher bankruptcy risk" belongs to the Canadian study). No exact
     neighbor magnitude verified; use qualitatively only.

3. Bankrate social media survey, fielded Aug 16-23, 2023 (YouGov): US adults spent an estimated $71 billion on impulse buys seen on
   social media in the prior 12 months; those who impulse-bought spent an average of $754 in those 12 months. 39% of social
   media users impulse-bought something they saw there in the past year; 57% of those regret at least one. (Ever: 48% / 68%.)
   Confirmed on Bankrate page and syndicated news (Sinclair stations).

4. Credit Karma survey, 2018 (Qualtrics): 39% of millennials surveyed spent money they didn't have to keep up with friends; of those,
   73% kept it secret; clothes most common (41%). Reported by Credit Karma and CNBC (Apr 20, 2018), ABC7. A later Credit Karma
   study put it at 48%. 2023 Credit Karma: 36% of Gen Z and millennials have a friend who drives them to overspend.

5. Origin: the phrase comes from Arthur "Pop" Momand's comic strip "Keeping Up with the Joneses," which began in 1913 (New York
   Globe). The Joneses themselves never appear in the strip; the McGinis family chase neighbors who are never shown.
   (Wikipedia; phrases.org.uk; wordorigins.org.) Momand first considered "the Smiths."

## Surprising fact
When a neighbor wins the lottery, the people next door become MORE likely to go bankrupt, and the bankrupt neighbors hold more
VISIBLE stuff (houses, cars), not more hidden stuff. Bonus: the Joneses never even appear in the comic that named them.

## Worked example (our math)
Bankrate average social-media impulse spend: $754 in 12 months (Aug 2023 survey).
$754 x 10 years = $7,540 (no interest, no inflation; our math, illustrative).
