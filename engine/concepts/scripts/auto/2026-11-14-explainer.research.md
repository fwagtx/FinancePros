# 2026-11-14-explainer research: Unemployment insurance: how it works and who pays for it

## Funding
- Federal-state system. Employers pay two taxes: federal FUTA and state unemployment tax (SUTA/SUI).
- FUTA (IRS Form 940; IRS Topic 759): 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages; employers that pay state UI taxes in full and on time get a credit of up to 5.4%, so the net rate is 0.6% = $42 per employee per year (0.006 x 7,000). Full rate without credit: 6.0% x 7,000 = $420.
- FUTA wage base: $7,000, set in 1983, unchanged since (Niskanen Center; CBO budget options). Chartable series: 1983-2026 constant at $7,000 (flat).
- Credit reduction states for 2025 tax year (loans outstanding): California, New York, Illinois, Connecticut, Ohio, New Jersey, Massachusetts -> employers there pay more.
- Workers: only Alaska, New Jersey and Pennsylvania also require employee contributions (DOL; Paychex). So in 47 states (and DC), workers pay nothing directly.
- FUTA pays for administration, federal share of extended benefits, and loans to states; state taxes fund regular benefits.

## Benefits
- For workers unemployed through no fault of their own who meet state earnings and job-search rules.
- Maximum weekly benefit 2026: lowest Mississippi $235; highest Washington $1,208 (claims filed on/after July 5, 2026; WA ESD).
- Duration: most states up to 26 weeks; some shorter, e.g. Florida 12 weeks.
- Taxes: unemployment benefits are taxable federal income, reported on Form 1099-G; you can choose 10% federal withholding with Form W-4V (IRS).

## Worked example
- Federal: 6.0% x $7,000 = $420; minus 5.4% credit ($378) = $42 per worker per year.
- A worker getting Washington's max for 26 weeks: 1,208 x 26 = $31,408 (before taxes); in Mississippi: 235 x 26 = $6,110.

## Surprise
In 47 states workers pay nothing directly for UI; and the federal tax base ($7,000) hasn't changed since 1983.
