# Research brief: Scam alert: brushing (Hot Take, posts Oct 22, 2026)

## What it is
- Brushing: a seller ships a cheap item to a real address so it can post a "verified purchase" review, often with a fake account in the recipient's name (FTC consumer guidance; Norton; USPIS "Brushing Scam" page).
- The address and name often came from leaked or bought data, so a brushing package can signal your info is out there (USPIS, Norton).

## The new twist: QR codes
- FBI IC3 PSA (July 31, 2025, PSA250731): criminals send unsolicited packages with a QR code that prompts recipients for personal and financial info, or installs malware that steals data from the phone. Advice: do not scan QR codes from unknown packages.
- USPIS: criminals are adding "quishing" (QR phishing) to brushing scams; cards say things like "scan to see who sent your gift".
- Local TV warnings were still running in Sept 2026 (KPRC Click2Houston, Sept 15, 2026).

## Your rights
- FTC and federal law (39 U.S.C. 3009): unordered merchandise sent by mail may be treated as a gift. You can keep it, use it or throw it away, with no obligation to pay or return it.

## Data series: total reported fraud losses (FTC Consumer Sentinel)
- 2022: $8.8B; 2023: $10.0B; 2024: $12.5B (+25%); 2025: $15.9B (record).

## Worked example (illustrative, hypothetical)
- $2 item + $4 shipping = $6 per fake "verified" review. 100 reviews x $6 = $600.

## Steps
- Don't scan the QR code. Check your online shopping accounts for unknown activity, change passwords, and report to the retailer and to ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

## Surprising fact
- You can legally keep it as a free gift; it's the code and your data that are the danger.
