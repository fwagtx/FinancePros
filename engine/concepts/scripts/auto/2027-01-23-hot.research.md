# Research brief: What happens if a company you own stock in goes bankrupt?
Slot: 2027-01-23-hot (Hot Take, posts Sat Jan 23, 2027). Researched Tue Nov 24, 2026. Evergreen; no timely news hook used.
Note: sec.gov and sipc.org could not be fetched directly from this session (network allowlist); facts below were confirmed via web search result excerpts of those official pages plus reputable outlets.

## Core rules (SEC / Investor.gov "Investor Bulletin: Bankruptcy for a Public Company"; FINRA)
- Chapter 7 = liquidation: company stops operating; a trustee sells assets and pays debts. Shareholders are generally left with nothing. (SEC bulletin; Investor.gov glossary)
- Chapter 11 = reorganization: company keeps operating and restructures its debts under a plan. (SEC bulletin)
- Priority (absolute priority rule): common stock ("equity") is "last in line" to receive what is available. FINRA capital-structure order: 1) secured bondholders, 2) unsecured bondholders, 3) subordinated debt, 4) preferred stockholders, 5) common stockholders. (SEC bulletin; FINRA "What a Corporate Bankruptcy Means for Shareholders")
- SEC: the plan of reorganization "will cancel the existing shares of common stock in most instances." New shares, if issued, usually go to creditors, including bondholders, as settlement. (SEC bulletin)
- Investor.gov glossary: regardless of chapter, common stock in a bankrupt company "is likely to be worthless."
- "Q" marker: once a company files, a "Q" may be added to its ticker symbol; old shares (if still traded, often OTC after delisting) keep the "Q"; new shares trade without it. (SEC bulletin / SEC "Q added" answer). Historical convention; current symbol practice can vary.

## SIPC (sipc.org "What SIPC Protects"; SEC fast answer; Investor.gov SIPC bulletin)
- SIPC protects customers when a SIPC-member brokerage firm fails and customer assets are missing: up to $500,000 per customer, including up to $250,000 for cash.
- SIPC does NOT protect against declines in market value, worthless securities, or bad advice. SIPC "does not bail out investors when the value of their stocks... falls for any reason." So a company's bankruptcy wiping out its stock is not covered.

## Real examples (dated)
- Lehman Brothers: filed Chapter 11 on Sept 15, 2008, with about $639 billion in assets, still the largest US bankruptcy filing. Plan confirmed Dec 6, 2011; per LBHI 8-K, on the Effective Date (March 6, 2012) all existing common and preferred stock was cancelled. (SEC EDGAR 8-K; History.com; Britannica)
- General Motors: filed Chapter 11 on June 1, 2009; NYSE delisted shares June 2, 2009 and they traded OTC; old GM became Motors Liquidation Company. Old GM stockholders recovered nothing; new GM stock went elsewhere (government, creditors). (Time, July 2009; Kiplinger June 2009)
- Hertz: filed Chapter 11 in May 2020. A March 2021 proposed plan gave shareholders nothing; a revised plan, confirmed June 10, 2021, paid creditors in cash in full and gave existing shareholders more than $1 billion of value (estimated about $7-$8 per share: $1.53 cash plus new stock and warrants). Exited June 30, 2021. Axios called it unusual: usually shareholders lose their entire investment. (Hertz release filed with SEC; Axios May 13, 2021; White & Case)

## Worked example (our math)
- 100 shares x $20 = $2,000 invested. If the plan cancels the shares: value = $0. Loss = $2,000 (100%).

## Surprising fact
- Even when the company survives Chapter 11 and keeps operating, old shareholders usually get nothing: the SEC says the plan cancels existing shares in most instances and new stock goes to creditors. And SIPC doesn't cover it.
