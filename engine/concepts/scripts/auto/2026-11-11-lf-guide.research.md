# 2026-11-11-lf-guide research: How to open and fund a Roth IRA, step by step

## Rules for tax year 2026 (IRS Notice 2025-67; IRS Publication 590-A/590-B)
- IRA contribution limit 2026: $7,500 total across ALL traditional + Roth IRAs; age 50+ catch-up $1,100 -> $8,600.
- You can contribute no more than your taxable compensation (earned income) for the year, if lower. A spousal IRA lets a working spouse fund an IRA for a non-working spouse (married filing jointly).
- Roth income phase-out (modified AGI), 2026: single/head of household $153,000-$168,000; married filing jointly $242,000-$252,000. Above the top: no direct Roth contribution. 2025 ranges were $150,000-$165,000 and $236,000-$246,000.
- Reduced amount inside the range (Pub 590-A): reduce the limit by (MAGI - bottom) / range width; round up to the next $10; if more than $0 but less than $200, you can contribute $200.
  Example: single, MAGI $160,500, under 50: (160,500 - 153,000) / 15,000 = 0.5 -> 7,500 x 0.5 = 3,750 reduction -> $3,750 allowed.
- Deadline: contributions for a tax year can be made until the tax-return due date, not counting extensions. For 2026 that is April 15, 2027. Tell the provider which year the contribution is for.
- 2027 limits: not announced as of Oct 1, 2026 (IRS usually announces in October/November); check IRS.gov.
- Withdrawals (Pub 590-B): Roth contributions (your own deposits) can be withdrawn at any time tax- and penalty-free (ordering rules: contributions come out first). Earnings are tax-free and penalty-free as a "qualified distribution" after age 59 1/2 AND 5 years since Jan 1 of the first year you contributed to any Roth IRA (some other exceptions: death, disability, up to $10,000 first home). Otherwise earnings can face income tax and a 10% additional tax.
- No required minimum distributions for the original owner of a Roth IRA.
- Excess contributions: 6% excise tax each year the excess stays in the account; fix by withdrawing the excess plus its earnings by the due date (including extensions) of the return.
- High earners: "backdoor Roth" = nondeductible traditional IRA contribution then conversion; pro-rata rule can make part of it taxable if you have other pre-tax IRA money.
- Funding an IRA is not investing it: deposits land in a cash/settlement (money market or sweep) position until you choose investments.
- Roth contributions are made with after-tax dollars; no deduction.

## Data
- ICI, "The Role of IRAs in US Households' Saving for Retirement, 2025": mid-2025, 44% of US households owned IRAs; Roth IRAs 27.8% (37.5 million households). 17% of all US households contributed to a traditional or Roth IRA for tax year 2024.

## Worked examples
- Maxing 2026: 7,500 / 12 = $625 a month; / 26 paychecks = $288.46; / 52 weeks = $144.23.
- Hypothetical (not a prediction): $7,500 a year for 30 years at a constant 7% annual return = 7,500 x ((1.07^30 - 1) / 0.07) = 7,500 x 94.4608 = $708,456; contributions $225,000; growth $483,456, tax-free if withdrawals are qualified.
- Phase-out example above: $3,750.
- Excess: $1,000 over the limit left in for 2 years -> 6% x $1,000 x 2 = $120 in excise tax.
